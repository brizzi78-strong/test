**CHAPTER 14**

## **Checking Boxes**

*"Man looketh on the outward appearance, but the LORD looketh on the heart."*
*— 1 Samuel 16:7*

**Laguna Beach, 2006**

After three days in Anaheim they moved me to Laguna Beach.

If you are going to be forced into rehab, you could do worse. It was the kind of place Hollywood people land when their publicists run out of excuses. Grounds somebody kept up. The ocean close enough to hear at night. Retired grandfathers who had been passing out in the garage, and younger guys who had burned through their twenties faster than they planned. I fit somewhere in the middle and told myself I fit nowhere.

I want to be clear about why I was there. Not because I had accepted anything. I still didn't believe I had a disease. I believed I had made a mistake, and the mistake had cost me a license and a career, and a judge had given me a choice between this and jail. Laguna Beach was the better option. That was the whole reason.

So I did what I had always done in a room I couldn't win. I performed. I went to every session. I sat in the circles. I said the things that sounded like progress and filled out the worksheets in full sentences. From the outside it probably looked like work. Inside I was counting days, and every one of them was a day closer to using again.

I made a friend there, Tristan. He had been on a soap opera. An absolute lunatic. He had done a lot of cocaine and hit a wall, and we clicked because we both came from lives that looked good from the street and were rotting inside. Between sessions we sat out on the grounds and talked about nothing and laughed. It felt like summer camp. That was the problem.

My therapist knew. Every day I told her the same thing. I can't get off the pills. I need them. I can't function without them. And every day she said the same thing back. You have to stop. You can't keep doing this. I heard her. I didn't believe her. The pills had been the answer so long I couldn't picture a version of myself that ran without them, and without them I was the scared kid from Mexico City who couldn't sit still and didn't believe he was enough.

I finished the program. I got the paperwork. I checked every box they put in front of me, and I walked out the same man who had walked in, with a certificate that said otherwise.

Three years later I would walk into a farmhouse in the Florida panhandle and say the one thing I never said in Laguna Beach. I am powerless. The difference between those two rooms wasn't the rooms. It was whether I meant it.

**Pensacola, 2007**

That's when Lou stepped in. He and Donna had just bought a house in Pensacola and offered me a place to land. I knew what it was: a rescue mission disguised as a move. "Come down here," he said. "Stay at home. Figure it out."

I moved to Pensacola in 2007 with no active license, still drinking, still convinced I could outwork the damage. My mother drove my car from California because I couldn't legally drive it myself. I let her. Before Lou and Donna arrived, I slept on the couch and chased job leads that disappeared the moment employers saw my driving record.

Florida gave me a loophole. Its computers didn't talk to California's. I passed the test, smiled for the camera, and walked out with a clean license. Laminated proof that I had beaten the system. Same man, new card.

One night before Lou and Donna made it down, I went out, and I drove home drunk.

I stopped at a red light and looked left. A cop. I looked right. Another cop. A man on a suspended license with liquor on his breath, boxed in on both sides, in a town where nobody knew him yet. I put both hands on the wheel and held my breath. I did not look at either of them. The light took forever.

It changed. They rolled on. I drove home.

People in the rooms talk about the moment of clarity. That red light was mine, and I want to be honest about what I did with it. Nothing. I didn't get sober that night. I didn't slow down for long. The late nights came back, the mornings that turned into afternoons, the résumé I kept updating and never sent. I would drive past Dad's house and see him in the driveway washing his car and think about pulling in. Pulling in meant questions about interviews I hadn't gone to. So I waved and kept driving.

Pensacola without work started to feel like a place I was hiding. When Aunt Nancey called and said come stay with me, I went.

Nancey lived in Boca Raton. She had always been the one who showed up. Wrestling matches, graduations, every moment that mattered, she was in the corner. But this was the first time it was the two of us. No Mom, no Chris, no occasion. Me at her door with nowhere better to go.

She knew my favorites were banana cream pie and chicken parm, so there was always whipped cream in the house and she made the pies. Not once. Regularly.

Nancey didn't drink. For that stretch, neither did I. The pills never stopped, but the drinking did, and that held as long as I was under her roof. Some nights she took me to meet her sober friends, not because she needed them, because she knew I did and wasn't going to make me go alone.

Most nights we just sat on the couch.

That is what I remember. Dateline. Forensic Files. 20/20. Hours of it, the two of us laughing at the things people do. Nancey and I are the same kind of person. We don't need to be doing something to feel like the night was worth it. Two people in a room, a show on, nobody performing anything. That was enough for her, and for a while it was enough for me. It was the most normal I had felt in years.

She knew I was in a dark place. She didn't try to fix it. She didn't ask me to explain it or show her progress. She sat with me until I was ready, and I wasn't ready.

I left. I got an apartment in West Palm Beach and I can't hand you a clean reason. The disease wanted more room than my aunt's couch gave it, and it sold me the move as a fresh start. In West Palm the drinking came back. I added cocaine. I all but stopped eating. My body started filing its own reports, malnutrition, metabolic syndrome, the machinery quietly failing while I told anyone who asked that I was fine.

Boca was the last soft place. West Palm Beach was where the disease finally got me alone.
