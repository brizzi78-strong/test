**CHAPTER 13**

## **My Brother's Keeper**

*"Am I my brother's keeper?"*
*— Genesis 4:9*

*Correcting a man's faults and telling him the truth about himself is the deepest compassion, and the first duty of anyone who serves him. But he will only hear it from someone who has earned the right.*
*— after Yamamoto Tsunetomo, Hagakure (c. 1716)*

**California, 2006**

Chris is three years younger than me. He wrestled at the University of Pennsylvania and had always been one of the steadiest people in my life. By 2006, I was supposed to be the older brother, but somewhere along the way he had taken over that role without ever asking for it.

There was a night before detox when I stayed at Chris's place and I was pretty messed up. I came out of the shower too fast and ran straight into the glass bathroom door, shattering the whole thing. I stood there looking at the mess, expecting a reaction. Chris looked at it and said, "Oh, that happens." That was the kind of grace he gave me during those years.

The detox in Anaheim was not something I chose because I had finally accepted that I was an addict. I hadn't. In my mind, my career was ending because I had made a mistake. My driver's license was gone, I needed money, and the alternative to doing what the court required was prison. I remember being told that because I was taking Xanax, three days of detox were facility protocol before I could go to the rehab in Laguna Beach. My mom, my dad, and Chris were all involved in getting me there, but I don't remember who made which call.

Chris drove. He had a way of keeping things normal even when the situation was anything but normal. He put on regular music and drove while I leaned the passenger seat back and tried to sleep. I had a little flip phone then, so there was nothing to stare at or scroll through. Mostly I closed my eyes and tried to detach. I wasn't thinking about recovery or what the next year of my life might look like. I was getting through the drive.

The detox house was a small square place on a corner near a little cul-de-sac, ordinary enough that you could have passed it without noticing anything different about it. Chris pulled up close enough that he didn't really have to park. I got out with an Abercrombie & Fitch backpack that had my things in it, including the pills.

Inside, an intake kid went through the backpack while Chris sat there. He found the Flintstones vitamin bottle I had used to hide pills. The whole thing was humiliating, but humiliation was low on the list of what I could process by then. I was dealing with the loss of my license, the possible end of my career, coming off drugs, and the fact that I was sitting in detox because prison was the other option. I was mostly numb.

Chris did not make a scene. He didn't lecture me or turn the bottle into some bigger moment than it already was. He sat there and stayed calm, which was what he had been doing through a period of my life when he had his own problems and very little room to express them because mine had become so large.

I remember Chris leaving and watching him pull away. I felt grateful for him, and I also remember thinking that nobody was going to have much sympathy for me for what I was about to go through. I had gotten myself there. There wasn't much more thought behind it than that.

The three days themselves are mostly a blur. I remember bunk beds and having the top bunk. The house felt crowded, maybe a dozen people in what seemed like a three-bedroom place. The other guys were polite. The staff treated me respectfully. We ate regular meals. Since I was coming off Xanax, they checked on me periodically because of the risk of seizures. I never had one.

I wasn't sitting there analyzing my addiction or thinking about what sobriety would mean. I was numb and trying to get by minute by minute. The pills wearing off were part of it, but there was also the life waiting for me when I got out — no license, a career I didn't know how to save, and a need to make money. I had no room for some bigger realization.

At some point in that stretch, after years of barely leaning on my faith at all, I remember saying to God, "Please, finish me off." It wasn't a polished prayer and I wasn't trying to understand what it meant. It was where I was.
