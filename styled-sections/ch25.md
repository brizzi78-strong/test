**CHAPTER 25**

## **Sobriety Meets Hospice**

*"I have fought a good fight, I have finished my course, I have kept the faith."*
*— 2 Timothy 4:7*

**Pensacola, 2015**

I saw things in the rooms that never leave you. Young people dying from drugs and alcohol. Not old men at the end of long lives, young people with everything ahead of them. I sponsored people who went back out. Many of them died. Some went to jail. I'd pour myself into someone, walk alongside them, answer their midnight calls, and then one day I'd get the call that they didn't make it.

Nothing in hospice is harder than a parent losing a child. The only reason I can stay in that room without flinching is that I've already sat with losses like that in recovery.

The very first time I was in a room with someone dying, I was too young to know that was what it was. My grandfather's mother was in a nursing home, near the end. I was a little boy with almost no English yet, Spanish still the only language I trusted. She lay against the pillows, white-haired and round-faced, and to me she looked like exactly one thing. Santa Claus. So I told her so. Ho, ho, ho. My mother said, Stop. I didn't understand the hush in the room, or that the woman in the bed was leaving, or that I would spend my life walking into rooms just like it. I only remember her face, and my mother's hand steering me out.

The first death I was in the room for wasn't a patient, and it came years before hospice was ever my job. It was Detroit Bob.

I met him at Courage at Noon during my first year sober. About eighty, bald, a scratchy voice, a face that looked permanently annoyed, like the world had let him down personally and he hadn't decided yet whether to forgive it. He smelled like Old Spice, just enough, never too much. His favorite line, and I heard it a hundred times: I'm not here to help any of you. I'm here to save my own butt. And you're listening to me? That's just a fringe benefit. People figured he was cynical, but Bob had done the work. He just wasn't going to perform it for an audience. And he had the one thing I could not stop staring at: decades sober, old, and at peace with all of it. I needed to understand how a person did that.

So we got close. We'd go to the diner together after meetings, me at thirty-one, him at eighty, the most unlikely pair in the place, and crack up. He'd tell stories that had no business being that funny, and whatever mess I was in he'd make smaller just by laughing at it, with me, not at me. Bob knew the difference. Then, years into the friendship, the cancer came. He knew it was going to take him, and he stayed sober anyway, a man at the very end of his life with every excuse in the world to pick up, choosing not to. He fought it for about six months, and somewhere in there he went on hospice, the first time that word ever meant someone I loved instead of a thing I would one day do for a living.

One afternoon he looked at me and said it straight out, the way he said everything. "I am so proud of the man I have seen you grow into over the past seven years. You have really blossomed. And sober looks good on you." Bob did not hand out lines like that. I have carried it ever since.

Bob wasn't the only man I loved who got cancer. Lou was diagnosed with stomach and esophageal cancer in the spring of 2015. He was seventy-three. He called me on a Tuesday. "Got some news," he said. "Surgery. And holistic stuff. No chemo. I'm not doing chemo." I knew better than to argue. "Okay," I said.

The surgery was brutal. Donna was there every day. I believe it's because of Donna that my father lived another ten years. She was a nurse; she understood his body in ways the rest of us didn't. She loved him completely and he loved her. He told me straight, more than once: Donna was the love of his life. My relationship with Donna was always centered on my dad. She was good to me, always. Who she is was exactly what Lou needed.

Lou came through the surgery. By December the scans were clean. The cancer stayed away for eight years — one scare in 2018, something on his liver, and nothing was ever done about it; it held. He was at my wedding in April 2019. Years that looked, from the inside, like a permanent gift. They weren't.

Bob didn't get that kind of time. Near the end, I went to see him, and it was nothing like the nursing-home room of my boyhood — this was a grown man actively dying, and everyone in the house knew it. The place was full. People he'd carried in the rooms, people who'd carried him, a steady stream coming to sit with him and say what they needed to say while he could still hear it. My first thought was a small, honest panic: how was I supposed to feel all of this without anything to take the edge off? I had never done grief without a buffer. The panic didn't last. What replaced it was gratitude, and a level calm, the plain fact of being there and not needing to be anywhere else. He told me once that sobriety wasn't something he fought every morning; it was a decision he had made a single time and never went back to revisit, and he carried it all the way to the edge of his life. Decades older than me, and still showing me how it was done.

I was in the room when he passed, in 2016. I had been around a lot by then, but not that, not the actual leaving.

It was a Sunday afternoon and the room had thinned out to four of us. His breathing had changed that morning, the nurse said, and once she said it I could hear it. Long pulls, and then a space, and then another pull. The spaces got longer. Every time one came, the four of us stopped breathing too, and every time he started again, somebody let out a little laugh, the kind you're ashamed of. His wife sat on the bed with her hand under his. Nobody talked. The Old Spice was still there. I had my hands folded in my lap like I was in church, and I remember noticing that and not knowing what else to do with them.

At some point the space didn't end. His wife knew before the nurse did. She said his name, not loud, and then she said, "Okay," and that was all. I looked at the clock because I'd seen people do that on television. Four twenty. Then I went and stood in the kitchen until she called me back in.

Afterward I stayed for his wife, a woman I loved, because there was nothing left to do but be there and not go. There was no fixing it, no deal to close, no right answer that would have bought him one more minute.

Around then, the recognition I'd chased at work finally came, and it barely registered. Someone forwarded an internal article. A colleague congratulated me. I skimmed it and closed the screen. Lou noticed. "You're successful again," he said. "I guess," I said. "You don't sound like it matters." "That's because it doesn't," I said. "Not the way it used to." He nodded.

I talked to Lou about Bob one evening. "I don't know why this is hitting me so hard," I said. "I did everything right." He didn't hesitate. "That's not why it's bothering you." He was right. Bob hadn't needed me to fix anything. He'd needed me in the room. I was being let into places where presence was the only thing left to offer, and something in me already knew how to do that. Years later, that became the whole job.
