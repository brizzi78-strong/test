**CHAPTER 16**

## **New Orleans**

*"Out of the belly of hell cried I, and thou heardest my voice."*
*— Jonah 2:2*

**New Orleans, Christmas 2008**

There was a night I almost slept under a bridge.

The rescue had bought me time, and I used it to get worse. Christmas 2008, I went to New Orleans to spend the holiday with my mother.

Before I went out, she gave me maybe five hundred dollars. I told her I was going to have a good time.

I burned through all of it that night.

At some point I looked around and had no idea where I was. No idea how to get back to the hotel. No money for a cab. Nothing.

I kept walking until the neighborhood changed. Dark streets. Nobody around. The kind of place where, even drunk, you know you shouldn't be.

I found a bridge and thought, maybe I'll sleep here. Five hundred dollars gone in a few hours, and the underside of a bridge looked like my best option.

I tried to find a cab. Tried to talk somebody into a ride. Nothing worked.

So I ran. Back through the neighborhood, in the dark, sprinting until I saw something I recognized.

I made it back to the hotel around four or five in the morning.

My mother was awake. She looked at me and knew. My eyes gave me away. They always did.

"Something is very wrong with you," she said.

I tried to brush it off. Change the subject. But she saw it. I could still lie with my mouth. My eyes had stopped lying for me.

The next two weeks were pure panic. Not fear of dying — fear of being caught. I moved through the days certain people were talking about me in rooms I wasn't in. They were. My mother and Lou, divorced twelve years, were coordinating a rehab intervention because their son was dying and that outranked everything.

Then Chris called. I was on the couch at my dad's house, the house the U-Haul had brought me back to. It started as an ordinary call — small talk, a lot of it, the way we always talked. He was calm. Somewhere in the small talk I got up and took the phone into the bedroom. Then, in the same even voice, he said it: "Admit you're addicted to drugs, or admit you have a mental-health problem that needs treatment. Pick one. No third door."

What I remember most is that I didn't feel judged. My brother had just handed me the two hardest sentences of my life, and there wasn't an ounce of verdict in either one. That's what left me nowhere to stand. Anger I could have fought.

My surrender wasn't clean. I want to tell it the way it happened.

After Chris hung up, the first thing I did was go to the pharmacy and fill my last Adderall script. Not to use it. That's what I told myself. I filled it because a bottle in my hand felt better than no bottle, and because the disease negotiates right up to the door of its own funeral.

Then I drove back to my dad's house with the bottle in my pocket.

He was in the kitchen. I walked in and took it out and held it toward him. For a second he didn't move. I had never seen that look on him. Lou Brizzi did not get caught off guard, and he was caught. He looked at the bottle and then at me.

"Dad," I said. "I'm ready to do this."

He took it. He turned it over once in his hand like he was checking that it was real.

"I'm proud of you," he said. "I'll get rid of these."

That was it. No speech. He didn't ask what had changed or why now or whether I meant it this time. He had the bottle and he had my word, and he was going to act on both before I could take either one back.

Handing him that bottle was the first honest thing my hands had done in years.

I never saw those pills again. I never asked what he did with them.

My surrender wasn't clean. Before I walked in to give up, I filled one last Adderall prescription. Then I handed the bottle to Lou and said the words: "I surrender. I'm gonna go."
