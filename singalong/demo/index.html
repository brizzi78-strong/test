How a circle runs

Every circle follows the same 30–40 minute arc. In memory care the routine is the program — residents who can’t keep track of “Tuesday at 2” can still feel “this is the singing time” when the gathering song starts.

  1. GatherHail, Hail, the Gang’s All Here — the same opener every session, sung while everyone settles.
  2. GreetAround the circle, each resident greeted by name.
  3. Core songsFamiliar songs on the week’s theme, words on the shared screen. Slower and lower than the recordings — leave room for residents’ voices.
  4. MoveOne up-tempo number with a simple motion: clap, sway, shakers and scarves in hand.
  5. CloseTill We Meet Again — a real goodbye song that promises return. End calm, not on a peak, and serve coffee in the same room after.

How we treat people in the room

Invite, never quiz. “I love this one” — not “Do you remember this?” Recognition without the test.
Every response counts. Humming, tapping, a look up — being in the room while the music plays is the point.
Tears are usually the music reaching someone. We don’t stop the song; we move closer, and we sit with them after.

The four-week cycle

What this asks of a team

One facilitator who never leaves the front, plus an aide for every four or five residents. Two circles a week plus a twenty-minute weekly review — about 1.5 staff-hours, on any TV and any tablet. Logging is one tap per song, after the singing, never instead of it.

Why these songs are free to use

Every song in the shared songbook was published before 1931, so the music and these lyrics are public domain — no licensing fees, no per-site subscription. Recordings stay whatever the facility already has the right to play. That rule costs us the 1940s hits this generation danced to — those live in each resident’s one-to-one listening, from the facility’s own music, where no lyric sheet is needed.

The people we sing with

Musical background only — never diagnoses, medications, or care-plan notes. The intake questions that matter: What was playing when they were 15–25? What was sung at home — at church, synagogue, temple, or mosque, or wherever singing happened? What was their first-dance song?

Start the sing-along

Past sing-alongs

Songs that reach this resident float to the top; a song that upset someone is flagged and rested. These are activity notes for choosing next week’s songs — not medical records, and never a score on a person.

Sessions

For families

Can I come? Yes. Family are welcome in the circle, and a daughter who knows all the words is the best co-facilitator there is.

Can I bring their music? Please. Their records, the songs from their wedding, the hymn their mother sang — that’s exactly what the one-to-one listening is made of, and there’s a conversation we’d love to have with you about it.

Will you tell me how it went? That’s what the notes are for: so someone can tell you “she came in restless and left calm, and she sang every word of Danny Boy.”

What if they say no, or cry? Then they say no — nobody is made to participate, and sitting near the music counts. Tears usually mean a song reached them; we stay close, let it finish, and sit with them after.

What we write down, plainly

Which songs were sung and how your person responded, plus how they seemed before and after. These are activity notes: no diagnoses, no medications, nothing shared outside the care team. You can ask to see them, and you can ask us to stop keeping them.
