Built for the memory-care neighborhood

Not an activity-room quiz. A room full of people singing.

Licensed platforms are built for the assisted-living activity room — trivia, reminiscence prompts, a recorded voice. In late-stage memory care a trivia question is a failure waiting to happen. This program is built on three rules instead.

Invite, never quiz.

I love this one — not Do you remember this? Recognition without the test.

Every response counts.

Humming, tapping, a look up. Being in the room while the music plays is the point.

Tears are the music reaching someone.

We don’t stop the song. We move closer, let it finish, and sit with them after.

Dignity on the shared screen. The TV shows only the words. A resident’s name, history, and how they responded stay on the facilitator’s tablet — never in front of the room or a visiting family.

How a circle runs

The same arc every time, because in memory care the routine is the program.

Residents who can’t keep track of “Tuesday at 2” can still feel “this is the singing time” when the gathering song starts. Thirty to forty minutes, four to ten residents, coffee in the same room after.

  1. Gather

    “Hail, Hail, the Gang’s All Here” — the same opener every session, sung while everyone settles.

  2. Greet

    Around the circle, each person greeted by name. Never re-greet latecomers from the front.

  3. Core songs

    Four songs on the week’s theme, words on the screen. Slower and lower than the record — leave room for their voices.

  4. Move

    One up-tempo number with one motion: clap, sway, shakers and scarves.

  5. Close

    “Till We Meet Again” — a goodbye that promises return. End calm, not on a peak.

Why the songs are free

Every song in the shared songbook was published before 1931, so the music and the words are public domain in the United States. No licensing fees, no per-site subscription, and the words can be printed and copied freely.

What that rule costs — and how the program answers it

It costs the 1940s and ’50s hits this generation danced to. Those aren’t missing; they have their own time. The shared circle uses the songs a whole room can sing together — school songs, church songs. Each resident’s own era — the Sinatra, the Hank Williams, the boleros — happens one to one, from the facility’s own library, where no lyric sheet is needed and no license is triggered.

The words are on the screen

Every song ships with its lyrics, sized for a TV across the room, plus a printable one-page circle sheet with the run order, the first line of each song as a cue, and the words overleaf — so the circle still happens when the Wi-Fi doesn’t.

What it asks of a team

Honest about the staffing, honest about the evidence

  • Who leadsOne facilitator who can carry a tune and never leaves the front — a cappella or with the building’s own recordings.
  • Who helpsOne aide for every four or five residents. The aide watches faces; a CNA is reachable.
  • TimeTwo circles a week plus a twenty-minute weekly review — about 1.5 staff-hours.
  • EquipmentThe TV already in the room and any tablet. Nothing to install, nothing to license.
  • LoggingOne tap per song, after the singing, never instead of it. Participation lands in the activity record as the circle runs.

If your building doesn’t have someone who can lead a song, a recorded-lead platform like SingFit is the right answer — and we’ll tell you so.

What the evidence actually says

Music is one of the most widely used non-drug approaches in dementia care, and the best current evidence — a 2025 Cochrane review — finds that music-based sessions reliably improve engagement and pleasure, and likely help depressive symptoms. The same evidence says they likely do not reduce agitation.

So this program doesn’t promise agitation reduction. It promises forty minutes twice a week where residents are present and singing, a record of who responded to what, and something specific to tell a family afterward.

What we write down, plainly

Which songs were sung and how each person responded, plus how they seemed before and after. Musical background only — never diagnoses, medications, or care-plan notes. Activity notes, not a chart, and never a score on a person.

For families

Come. Bring their music. Ask how it went.

Can I come?

Yes. Family are welcome in the circle, and a daughter who knows all the words is the best co-facilitator there is.

Can I bring their music?

Please. Their records, the songs from their wedding, the hymn their mother sang — that’s exactly what the one-to-one listening is made of.

Will you tell me how it went?

That’s what the notes are for: so someone can tell you “she came in restless and left calm, and she sang every word of Danny Boy.”

What if they say no, or cry?

Then they say no — nobody is made to participate, and sitting near the music counts. Tears usually mean a song reached them; we stay close.
