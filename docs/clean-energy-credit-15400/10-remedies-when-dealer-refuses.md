# Chapter 10 (Practitioner Annex). Legal position when a dealership refuses to file

*This chapter is analysis, not legal or tax advice. State law varies materially and the facts of an individual transaction control. Anyone with a live claim should consult counsel admitted in their jurisdiction and verify the authorities cited here against primary sources.*

## 10.1 The short answer

The buyer has **no federal remedy against the dealer** and, on the IRS side, a **weak and largely untested position**. The real remedies are in state contract and consumer protection law, and the most effective pressure is not litigation at all but the dealer's two licenses: its state motor vehicle dealer license and its IRS Energy Credits Online registration.

That asymmetry is the whole problem. The obligation is federal, the sanction for breaching it falls on the buyer, and the only enforceable rights the buyer has arise under state laws that were written for odometer fraud and financing markups rather than for a tax filing.

## 10.2 First, distinguish two situations

**Situation A: the dealer can still file and won't.** The dealer is registered in Energy Credits Online and the portal will still accept the submission. After the 2025 reopening the IRS accepted late reporting for 2024 transactions on an open-ended basis, and reporting for pre-cutoff acquisitions remained necessary into 2026. This is a leverage problem, not a legal one. The fix costs the dealer fifteen minutes and the entire objective is to make those fifteen minutes cheaper than the alternative.

**Situation B: the dealer cannot file.** It never registered, or its registration lapsed, or it has closed. New registration for the clean vehicle program closed on September 30, 2025, so a seller that never enrolled may now have no route to file at all. Here the credit is likely gone permanently and the only question is money damages against the dealer.

These two facts are easy to mistake for a contradiction and are not one. The **filing** window was reopened and left open; the **registration** window was closed by the termination. An already-registered dealer can still cure a missing report for a qualifying pre-cutoff sale. A dealer that never enrolled cannot enroll now in order to cure. The buyer's entire practical position turns on which side of that line the seller sits, which is why §10.2 puts the registration question first.

Establish which situation applies before doing anything else. Ask the dealer directly, in writing, whether it is registered in IRS Energy Credits Online and whether a report for the VIN was submitted and accepted. The answer determines the entire strategy.

## 10.3 Federal tax law: why there is no claim against the dealer

**The obligation runs to the government, not to the buyer.** Section 30D(d)(1)(H) conditions the vehicle's qualification on the seller furnishing a report to the taxpayer and to the Secretary. The regulations define the seller report as one provided in the manner prescribed by guidance published in the Internal Revenue Bulletin, and Rev. Proc. 2023-33, with later procedures including Rev. Proc. 2024-26 on updating and rescinding reports, supplies that manner. Nothing in that structure creates a private cause of action. The Internal Revenue Code creates express private rights of action sparingly, and none of them fits: §7431 covers unauthorized disclosure by the government, §7433 covers unauthorized collection actions by the IRS, and §7434 covers the willful filing of a **fraudulent** information return with respect to payments purportedly made to another person. A failure to file is not a fraudulent filing, and a seller report is not a payment information return. A §7434 theory is available in principle where a dealer files a knowingly false report, and it is close to hopeless where the dealer simply filed nothing.

**Nor is there a claim against the IRS.** The unperformed duty is the dealer's, so mandamus does not lie against the agency. The agency's own position, expressed in the 2025 reopening, is accommodating: it kept the window open so dealers could cure. It just cannot cure for them.

**The buyer's own tax posture is weak but not empty.** A buyer denied the credit can decline to accept the denial: claim it, receive the notice, and litigate in the Tax Court in a deficiency posture, or pay and sue for refund. Three arguments exist, and none has been tested in a published decision as far as this research could determine.

1. *Substantial compliance.* Where the dealer handed the buyer a complete written report containing every required element and simply failed to transmit it, the buyer received what the statute says they must receive. The doctrine has been applied to procedural and directory requirements and refused for conditions the courts read as substantive. The government's answer is that §30D(d)(1)(H) is definitional: it makes the report an attribute of the qualifying vehicle, and no amount of good faith turns a non-qualifying vehicle into a qualifying one. That is the stronger textual argument.

2. *The three-day rule as a substantive eligibility condition.* The statute says nothing about timing. The three-calendar-day deadline comes from a revenue procedure. There is a serious argument that a filing deadline set by sub-regulatory guidance cannot by itself destroy a statutory entitlement. The IRS has substantially mooted this by accepting late reports, which is one reason it has never been litigated.

3. *Reasonable cause.* Largely unavailable. Reasonable cause excuses penalties; it does not manufacture eligibility for a credit whose statutory definition is unmet.

**Why no case law exists.** The amount in dispute is $7,500 or less. That is far below the threshold at which it is rational to litigate a federal tax case, even in the Tax Court's small case procedure, where representation costs would exceed the recovery. So the claims are individually too small to litigate, the doctrine never develops, and the agency's informal accommodation is the only law there is. This is a structural feature of consumer tax credits and it is worth naming: making a benefit small and dispersed makes its legal boundaries unenforceable in practice.

## 10.4 State law: where the enforceable rights are

**Breach of contract.** The strongest theory when the paperwork supports it. Look at the buyer's order, the purchase agreement, any clean vehicle addendum, and any written communication. Where the dealer promised to file the report, or where the transaction was priced on the express footing that the buyer would receive the credit, the failure to file is a breach and the measure of damages is the lost credit, up to $7,500 or $4,000. Foreseeability is not a real obstacle: the entire transaction was structured around the credit and the dealer knew it.

**Negligent misrepresentation and fraud.** Available where the dealer affirmatively represented that the report had been filed, or that the buyer would receive the credit, and the representation was false when made or made with reckless indifference. Fraud opens the door to punitive damages in many states and defeats contractual limitation clauses, but it carries a heightened pleading standard and requires evidence of the representation.

**State unfair and deceptive acts and practices statutes.** Every state has one, and these are the most useful tools in the box: Massachusetts ch. 93A, California's Consumers Legal Remedies Act and Unfair Competition Law, New York General Business Law §349, the Texas Deceptive Trade Practices Act, and their analogues. Most cover motor vehicle sales expressly and many provide **attorney's fees and multiple damages**. That is the decisive feature. A standalone $7,500 contract claim is not economically viable for a lawyer to take; the same claim with statutory fee-shifting is. Several also require a pre-suit demand letter, which is a formality worth complying with because it frequently resolves the matter on its own.

**Small claims court.** Often the right answer in Situation B and a credible threat in Situation A. Limits vary by state, commonly between $5,000 and $12,500, with some higher and some lower, so $7,500 is within reach in many places and $4,000 in nearly all. No lawyer is needed, filing costs are trivial, and a dealership that will ignore a customer's phone calls will usually respond to a summons.

**Do not rely on the FTC's CARS Rule.** The Combating Auto Retail Scams Rule was reported vacated by the Fifth Circuit in early 2025 on procedural grounds, and in any event §5 of the FTC Act carries no private right of action. Complaints to the FTC are worth filing as a matter of record, not as a remedy.

## 10.5 The pressure that actually works

Litigation is the fallback. Three forms of leverage resolve these matters faster.

**The state dealer license.** Motor vehicle dealers are licensed by a state board, DMV, or dealer commission, and licensure is conditioned on lawful and honest dealing. A dealer's license is worth vastly more than $7,500. A complaint to the licensing authority, copied to the dealer's general manager, is the single most effective step available to a consumer, and it costs nothing.

**The IRS registration.** Participation in Energy Credits Online is a privilege the IRS can suspend or revoke for noncompliance, and a dealer that took advance payments has an ongoing federal relationship it does not want examined. For transactions still within the reporting window, pointing out that the buyer intends to report the dealer's non-filing to the IRS concentrates attention.

**The state attorney general and the manufacturer.** Consumer protection divisions in most states run complaint mediation programs that dealers take seriously. For franchised dealers, the manufacturer's customer relations channel is a separate and often effective route, because dealer performance metrics reach the franchisor.

## 10.6 A practical sequence

1. **Assemble the record.** Purchase agreement, buyer's order, financing documents, any Form 15400 copy received, the VIN, the delivery date, and every communication about the credit.
2. **Determine registration status in writing.** Ask whether the dealer is registered in IRS Energy Credits Online and whether a report for the VIN was submitted and accepted. Get the answer by email.
3. **Escalate inside the dealership.** The ECO account usually sits with one person, and staff turnover is the most common cause of non-filing. The general manager or controller can often fix in an afternoon what the salesperson cannot.
4. **Send a written demand.** State the VIN, the date of sale, the credit amount, the request that the report be filed, and a deadline. Where the state's UDAP statute requires a pre-suit demand, comply with its form and timing.
5. **File the licensing and agency complaints.** State dealer licensing authority, state attorney general consumer division, and the IRS. These are free and they run in parallel with everything else.
6. **Preserve the tax position.** File the return on time. If the report is later filed, amend within the limitations period, generally three years from filing or two years from payment. Do not let the tax clock run out while the dispute proceeds.
7. **Sue.** Small claims for speed, or a UDAP claim with fee-shifting where the conduct supports it and the amount justifies counsel.

## 10.7 The mirror image: when the dealer wants money back from the buyer

A separate scenario deserves flagging, because the parties' positions reverse.

Where the buyer made a transfer election, received the credit as a price reduction at the counter, and the dealer then failed to file and lost its advance payment, the loss is the **dealer's**. The buyer already has the benefit. Some dealers in this position have sought to recover from customers. Whether they can depends entirely on the contract: absent a clear clawback provision, there is generally no basis to charge a customer for the dealer's own filing failure, and attempting to collect may itself be actionable under state consumer protection law. A buyer receiving such a demand should not pay it without reviewing the purchase documents with counsel.

The underlying tax rule cuts the same way. A transferred credit was not recaptured from the buyer merely because their tax liability was insufficient to absorb it. Recapture ran against the buyer where the buyer's own attestations failed, principally the income limits, and against the dealer where the dealer's conduct was at fault.

## 10.8 What this shows about the design

Every remedy described above is a workaround. The buyer's federal entitlement is enforced, if at all, through a state consumer statute enacted for unrelated purposes, or through a licensing board with no connection to the Internal Revenue Code, or through a small claims judgment a dealer pays to make a nuisance go away. Nowhere in the system is there a mechanism by which a person who satisfied every condition Congress wrote can obtain the benefit Congress granted, over the objection or inaction of a private party with no stake in the outcome.

That is the concrete form of the risk misallocation set out in Chapter 5, and it is the reason the first design principle in Chapter 8 is stated as an absolute: where a third party's filing is a condition of a public benefit, the beneficiary must have an independent route to establish entitlement. The clean vehicle credits never had one, and the credits were terminated before anyone built it.
