# Chapter 2. Statutory genealogy, 2008–2025

## 2.1 Origins: a credit designed for a market that did not exist

Section 30D entered the Code through the Energy Improvement and Extension Act of 2008, part of the same legislation that carried the financial rescue of that autumn. It was written for a product almost nobody could yet buy. The American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009 rewrote it into the form that would persist for thirteen years: a base credit of $2,500 plus $417 for each kilowatt-hour of battery capacity above five kilowatt-hours, capped at $7,500, non-refundable, and subject to a phase-out that began after a manufacturer sold its 200,000th qualifying vehicle in the United States.

Three features of that design deserve attention because they shaped everything after.

**It was non-refundable.** A household with $3,000 of federal income tax liability received $3,000, not $7,500, and could not carry the remainder forward. This is the mechanical source of the distributional pattern documented by Borenstein and Davis, who found that the top income quintile received on the order of 90% of vehicle credit dollars in the program's first decade. The credit was not means-tested until 2023; it was liability-tested from the start, which is means-testing by a cruder instrument and in the wrong direction.

**It was claimed on a return.** The buyer paid full price at the dealership and recovered the credit months later, if their liability permitted, after their preparer completed Form 8936. The subsidy was deferred, uncertain in amount for anyone unsure of their year-end liability, and invisible at the moment of the purchase decision. The behavioral economics literature predicts that such a subsidy is worth substantially less than its face value to the marginal buyer, and Chapter 4 develops that point.

**It phased out per manufacturer, not per market.** The 200,000-vehicle cap penalized the firms that responded most vigorously. By 2022, buyers of vehicles from the two largest domestic EV producers received nothing, while buyers of vehicles from manufacturers that had barely entered the market received the full $7,500. As industrial policy this is close to a perverse instrument, and repealing it was among the least controversial elements of the 2022 reform.

Documentation in this era was correspondingly thin. The seller's obligation was to furnish the buyer a written report containing prescribed information, and manufacturers certified qualifying models to the IRS. Nothing was transmitted contemporaneously; nothing was matched against anything; the VIN did not function as a national register key.

## 2.2 The Inflation Reduction Act: four reforms in one section

Section 13401 of the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 restructured §30D more thoroughly than any prior amendment. It is useful to separate the changes into four programs that happened to share a section number, because they answer to different objectives and, as Chapter 6 argues, partly defeat one another.

**A decarbonization program.** The per-manufacturer 200,000-vehicle cap was repealed for vehicles sold after 2022. This is the only change unambiguously aimed at maximizing electric vehicle adoption, and it was the change most directly reversed in effect by everything else in the section.

**A distributional program.** Adjusted-gross-income limits were imposed: $300,000 for joint filers, $225,000 for heads of household, $150,000 for others, tested against the lesser of the current or prior year. Manufacturer's suggested retail price caps were added at $80,000 for vans, sport utility vehicles, and pickup trucks and $55,000 for other vehicles. Together these were an attempt to correct the Borenstein–Davis pattern by statute. They are also the source of a great deal of the classification litigation and administrative grief of 2023, since whether a given crossover was an "SUV" moved its cap by $25,000.

**An industrial policy program.** The credit was split into two independent halves of $3,750. One required a threshold percentage of the battery's critical minerals to be extracted or processed in the United States or a free-trade-agreement partner, or recycled in North America. The other required a threshold percentage of battery components to be manufactured or assembled in North America. Both thresholds escalated annually. A separate final-assembly-in-North-America requirement applied to the vehicle itself from the date of enactment, August 16, 2022, which stranded a number of models overnight. Foreign-entity-of-concern exclusions took effect for battery components in 2024 and for critical minerals in 2025.

**An administrative program.** Two changes, easy to overlook beside the others, did the work this dissertation is about. First, §30D(d)(1)(H) conditioned eligibility on the seller furnishing a report to the taxpayer and to the Secretary containing prescribed information, including the VIN and the maximum credit allowable. Second, §30D(g) permitted the taxpayer to elect to transfer the credit to the registered dealer, effective for vehicles placed in service after December 31, 2023, with the dealer to be paid an advance by the Treasury. The seller report ceased to be a courtesy document and became a jurisdictional prerequisite; the transfer election turned the dealer into a disbursing agent of the federal government.

## 2.3 The companion credits

**Section 25E**, the previously-owned clean vehicle credit, was new in the Inflation Reduction Act and effective for vehicles acquired from 2023. It equaled 30% of the sale price up to a maximum of $4,000, applied only to vehicles priced at $25,000 or less purchased from a licensed dealer, required a model year at least two years earlier than the year of sale, and imposed AGI limits of $150,000, $112,500, and $75,000 for joint, head-of-household, and other filers. A taxpayer could claim it only once every three years. Section 25E(f) carried the same transfer election as §30D(g).

Section 25E is the most interesting of the three from a distributional standpoint and the least studied. It was aimed squarely at households the new-vehicle credit could never reach, in a market segment where a $4,000 discount is a very large fraction of the transaction price, and it was the credit most dependent on point-of-sale delivery, because a buyer of a $20,000 used car is far more likely to be liquidity-constrained than a buyer of a $55,000 new one.

**Section 45W**, the qualified commercial clean vehicle credit, was also new and effective from 2023. It equaled the lesser of 15% of basis (30% for vehicles not powered by a gasoline or diesel internal combustion engine) or the incremental cost of the vehicle relative to a comparable conventional one, capped at $7,500 for vehicles under 14,000 pounds gross vehicle weight rating and $40,000 above. Critically, §45W carried none of §30D's AGI limits, MSRP caps, final assembly requirement, critical mineral or battery component thresholds, or foreign-entity-of-concern exclusions.

That asymmetry created the leasing channel. A leasing company that purchases a vehicle and leases it to a consumer is the owner and the taxpayer, the vehicle is business property, and §45W applied without any of the conditions Congress had spent months negotiating into §30D. Treasury confirmed that lessors could claim §45W on consumer leases. The share of new EVs leased rose from roughly 12% in 2023 to roughly 35% in the first quarter of 2024. Chapter 6 treats this as the central design failure of the 2022 reform, and the one that most directly undercut the industrial policy program.

## 2.4 The guidance layer, 2022–2024

Statutes of this complexity are executed through guidance, and three instruments matter here.

**Rev. Proc. 2022-42** established the initial procedures for manufacturer certification and for the seller reports required under §30D and §25E, in the pre-portal era of written reports furnished to buyers.

**Rev. Proc. 2023-33**, issued in October 2023 alongside proposed regulations, is the operative document for everything this dissertation studies. It unified the seller report requirements for §30D and §25E, established dealer and seller registration through IRS Energy Credits Online, set out the mechanics of the transfer election and the advance payment program, and fixed the timing rules: the seller files its report within three calendar days of the sale and furnishes a copy to the taxpayer, and a dealer must have been registered for a period before it becomes eligible to receive advance payments, reported as fifteen days. It applied to transfers of credits after December 31, 2023.

**The §30D regulations finalized in 2024**, including Treas. Reg. §1.30D-5 on transfer of the credit, converted the proposed rules into final ones, addressed dealer attestations, the treatment of advance payments exceeding the buyer's tax liability, recapture, and the consequences of ineligibility discovered after the fact.

The pattern across these instruments is worth naming. Congress wrote two sentences about seller reports and a subsection about transfer. The executive branch built from them a registration system, a real-time transmission requirement, a national VIN register, a payment system operating on a seventy-two hour reimbursement cycle, and an attestation regime binding a hundred thousand-odd sales staff at fifteen thousand dealerships. That is a large administrative structure resting on a thin statutory foundation, and Chapter 5 argues the thinness is visible in exactly the place where it hurt: nothing in the statute or the guidance told a buyer what to do when the dealer simply did not file.

## 2.5 Termination

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, Pub. L. No. 119-21, 139 Stat. 72, was signed on July 4, 2025. It terminated §30D, §25E, and §45W for vehicles **acquired after September 30, 2025**, and separately terminated or curtailed a set of neighboring provisions, including §30C for charging property after June 30, 2026, §25C and §25D for property placed in service after December 31, 2025, and §45L after June 30, 2026.

Two features of the termination shaped the market response.

**It used "acquired," not "placed in service."** IRS guidance issued after enactment took the position that a vehicle is acquired when a written binding contract is in place and a payment has been made, including a nominal down payment, so that a buyer who contracted and paid on or before September 30, 2025 could claim the credit even if delivery occurred later. This transformed the last week of September from a delivery deadline into a contracting deadline and enabled a larger pull-forward than a delivery rule would have.

**It gave the market not quite ninety days' notice.** Enactment on July 4 and termination on September 30 produced a compressed, fully anticipated, precisely dated demand shock. Every participant knew the date. The resulting September spike and October collapse are analyzed in Chapter 7.

The termination did not extinguish the administrative apparatus. Seller reports remained required for vehicles acquired on or before the cutoff, the reporting portal continued to accept them, and buyers who acquired before the deadline but took delivery afterwards still needed a valid Form 15400 to claim on a 2025 or 2026 return. The form outlived the credit, which is a fitting end for a document that had always mattered more than the statute admitted.

## 2.6 Summary

Across seventeen years Congress adjusted the amount of the clean vehicle credit once, in 2009, and thereafter adjusted eligibility repeatedly: who could claim, for which vehicles, sourced from where, at what income. Only once, in 2022, did it change how the money got to the buyer. That change is responsible for most of the observable difference between the pre-2024 and post-2024 program, and Chapters 3 and 4 examine its instrument.
