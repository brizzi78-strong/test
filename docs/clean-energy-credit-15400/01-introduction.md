# Chapter 1. Introduction: reading a tax expenditure through its paperwork

## 1.1 A form nobody meant to be important

Form 15400 is one page. It records a vehicle identification number, the battery capacity in kilowatt-hours, the model year, the sale price, the date of sale and the date the vehicle was placed in service, the maximum credit allowable, and the identities and taxpayer identification numbers of the seller and the buyer. There is a version for new vehicles and a version for previously-owned vehicles. A dealer generates it inside the IRS Energy Credits Online portal at the moment of sale, transmits it, and prints a copy for the customer. It has no deduction schedule, no worksheet, no signature block for a preparer. Judged by its face, it is a receipt.

Judged by what it did, it was the operating system of a multi-billion-dollar subsidy. Without an accepted Form 15400 keyed to a specific VIN and a specific named buyer, no clean vehicle credit could be claimed by anyone, in any amount, ever, for that vehicle. With one, a buyer could walk out of a dealership having paid $7,500 less than the sticker arithmetic implied, with the Treasury reimbursing the dealer directly. The form was the eligibility certificate, the duplicate-claim register, and the payment instruction, and it was all three at once because Congress had asked the tax system to do something the tax system is not built to do: pay a benefit at the moment of a private transaction rather than after the close of a tax year.

This dissertation is about what happens when that is attempted. The claim is not that Form 15400 was well or badly designed, though both can be argued. The claim is that the form and the machinery around it *were* the policy in every respect that turned out to matter, and that a reading of the clean vehicle credits that stops at the statute will misunderstand their take-up, their distribution, their failure modes, and their repeal.

## 1.2 The gap between the statute and the program

Consider the divergence. As a matter of statutory text, §30D in 2024 was a non-refundable personal credit of up to $7,500 against income tax liability, subject to adjusted-gross-income caps, manufacturer's suggested retail price caps, a North American final assembly requirement, and two independent $3,750 halves conditioned on critical-mineral and battery-component sourcing. Nothing in that description tells you that a household with no federal income tax liability at all could receive the full $7,500, in cash value, at the point of sale, and keep it. Yet that is what the transfer election in §30D(g) accomplished, and the regulations confirmed that the advance payment was not recaptured merely because the buyer's liability was too small to absorb the credit.

That single feature converted a credit whose distribution was mechanically regressive, because non-refundable credits are worth nothing to those without liability, into something a moderate-income household could actually use. It was not achieved by amending the credit's amount or its refundability in any conventional sense. It was achieved by moving the point of payment and inserting an intermediary, and it was documented by a form.

The same logic runs the other way. As a matter of statutory text, a buyer who satisfied every condition in §30D was entitled to the credit. As a matter of program operation, a buyer who satisfied every condition but whose dealer failed to transmit Form 15400 within three calendar days was told by the IRS that the credit could not be claimed. Thousands of people discovered this in the 2025 filing season. The statute said one thing; the administrative architecture said another; the administrative architecture won, until the agency reopened the reporting window under pressure from the dealer associations. The episode is the subject of Chapter 5, and it is the clearest evidence for the general proposition that in a point-of-sale regime the paperwork is not evidence of the entitlement, it constitutes it.

## 1.3 Why this object, and why now

Three features make the clean vehicle credits an unusually good case for studying delivery-system design.

**The instrument is fully observed from birth to death.** §30D was enacted in 2008, restructured in 2009, restructured again in 2022, given its point-of-sale apparatus in 2023 with effect from January 1, 2024, and terminated for vehicles acquired after September 30, 2025. §25E and §45W lived and died inside a single thirty-three month window. Few tax expenditures offer a complete life cycle at this resolution, with a discrete, dated, exogenous end.

**The design change and the outcome change are separated in time.** The eligibility rules were rewritten in August 2022 and took effect in stages through 2024. The delivery mechanism changed on January 1, 2024, with the eligibility rules largely held constant across that date. That is close to the structure of a clean policy experiment on the delivery channel itself, and it is rare.

**The termination is sharp.** The credit did not phase down, taper, or expire quietly at year end. It ended for vehicles acquired after a Tuesday in September, with a statutory acquisition rule that let buyers lock in with a written binding contract and a payment. The result is a spike and a cliff in the highest-frequency, best-measured consumer durable market in the country. Chapter 7 argues this is the single most identifiable event in the history of U.S. energy tax expenditures, and that the profession should treat it accordingly.

There is also a practical reason to write this in 2026 rather than later. The compliance tail of a terminated credit is long. Vehicles acquired under written binding contract on or before September 30, 2025 could be placed in service afterwards, and the seller report and recordkeeping obligations survive the credit that gave rise to them. Dealers, buyers, and examiners are still working through 2025 and 2026 returns. The administrative record is fresh and the participants remember.

## 1.4 The argument in outline

Chapter 2 traces the statutory genealogy from the Energy Improvement and Extension Act of 2008 to the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, showing that Congress repeatedly changed *who* qualified and rarely changed *how* the benefit was delivered, until it changed the delivery in 2022 and thereby changed everything else.

Chapter 3 is a close reading of Form 15400 itself: its contents, its predecessors, the three-day filing rule, the registration prerequisites, and its relationship to the taxpayer's own filings on Form 8936 and Schedule A of that form. The chapter argues that the form performs four distinct legal functions that Congress never separately authorized and that no single provision of the Code fully describes.

Chapter 4 treats the transfer election under §30D(g) and §25E(f) as a deliberate delivery-system redesign, and reads it against the economics of salience, liquidity constraints, and take-up. The theoretical prediction is that moving a subsidy from deferred and uncertain to immediate and certain raises its behavioral value well above its nominal value for exactly the households the program had previously missed.

Chapter 5 documents what broke. Roughly 12,200 sellers registered for the advance payment program and another 3,400 for reporting only; over 600,000 claims were processed on returns filed in calendar year 2024; and an unknown but substantial number of buyers were told their credits were unavailable because reports had never been filed. The chapter analyzes the allocation of risk between the parties and argues it was misallocated as a matter of both fairness and efficiency.

Chapter 6 addresses incidence and leakage. It reviews the evidence that consumers captured most of the subsidy but that a large fraction of recipients were inframarginal, revisits the finding that the top income quintile historically received close to 90% of vehicle credit dollars, and then examines the §45W leasing channel, which by early 2024 accounted for roughly a third of new EV acquisitions and which carried none of the income, price, or sourcing conditions Congress had written into §30D.

Chapter 7 turns to the termination and the data. It reports the September–October 2025 discontinuity and the 2026 partial recovery, distinguishes intertemporal substitution from the level effect, and specifies four empirical designs the surviving data would support.

Chapter 8 generalizes. The intermediary-administered benefit is now a recurring pattern in U.S. fiscal policy, appearing in elective pay and transferability across the Inflation Reduction Act's business credits and proposed repeatedly for home energy credits. The chapter derives design principles from the vehicle experience, of which the first is that the beneficiary must not bear the intermediary's compliance risk.

Chapter 9 concludes and sets out a research agenda.

## 1.5 What this dissertation does not claim

It does not claim that the clean vehicle credits were cost-effective per ton of carbon abated. The literature on that question is unsettled and the answer depends heavily on assumptions about counterfactual vehicle choice and grid composition that this study does not attempt to adjudicate.

It does not claim that the repeal was mistaken. It claims that the repeal was made easier by the same salience that made the credit work, which is a statement about political economy, not about optimal policy.

It does not claim that dealers behaved badly. The evidence suggests most complied with an unfamiliar obligation on a short deadline through an unfamiliar portal, and that the failures were concentrated in the first year and in smaller operations, which is the ordinary pattern when a new administrative burden is imposed on a dispersed population of small businesses. The point of Chapter 5 is that the system should not have been designed to fail catastrophically for the buyer when the seller made an ordinary mistake.
