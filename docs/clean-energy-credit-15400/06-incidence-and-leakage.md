# Chapter 6. Incidence, distribution, and the leasing channel

## 6.1 Three questions that are usually conflated

Public argument about the clean vehicle credits ran together three separate questions, and separating them is most of the analytical work.

1. **Incidence.** Of each subsidy dollar, how much ends up with the buyer and how much is captured by manufacturers and dealers through higher prices?
2. **Marginality.** Of the buyers who received the subsidy, how many would have bought an electric vehicle anyway?
3. **Distribution.** Where in the income distribution did the subsidy dollars land?

A program can do well on one and badly on another. The clean vehicle credits did reasonably well on incidence, poorly on marginality, and badly on distribution until 2023, after which the picture becomes genuinely contested.

## 6.2 Incidence: consumers kept most of it

The consistent finding of the vehicle-subsidy literature is that buyers capture most of the money. Sallee's study of the Toyota Prius credit found that buyers captured essentially the entire subsidy, a result driven by the elastic supply of a mass-produced vehicle over the relevant horizon. Muehlegger and Rapson, using quasi-experimental variation from California's low- and middle-income rebate programs, estimate pass-through to consumers in the range of 70% to 100%.

The intuition is that with elastic supply and competitive retail, a per-unit subsidy on the demand side moves quantity rather than producer surplus. Where supply is constrained, whether by battery capacity, allocation, or a hot model, the prediction reverses and the seller captures more, which is why the incidence question cannot be answered once and for all; it depends on the period and the model.

Point-of-sale transfer complicates the analysis in an interesting way. It raises the subsidy's salience to the buyer, which strengthens the demand response and, holding supply constant, transfers *more* surplus to sellers, not less. Against that, it also makes the subsidy conspicuous in the negotiation, and a discount itemized on a government form is harder to quietly reclaim through the sale price than a credit the buyer will collect in April. Which effect dominates is an open empirical question that the January 2024 transition is well suited to answer, and nobody has answered it.

The suggestive evidence points toward buyer capture. When the credit disappeared in October 2025, average electric vehicle discounts rose to roughly $13,161 as manufacturers moved to replace part of the withdrawn subsidy from their own margins. Sellers replacing a subsidy they had captured would be an odd response; the behavior is more consistent with the subsidy having been supporting transaction volume at prices the market would not otherwise bear.

**A third channel the standard framework misses.** Incidence is conventionally measured as the split of a subsidy between buyer and seller, holding the product constant. The October 2025 evidence shows that assumption failing. One week after termination Tesla introduced a Model Y "Standard" at $39,990, roughly $5,000 below the renamed trim above it, and reached that price by removing content: a fifteen-speaker audio system cut to seven, no AM/FM radio, no rear entertainment screen, textile in place of vegan leather, manual vents, mirrors, and steering wheel adjustment, no ventilated front seats or heated rear seats. Chapter 11 §11.4.6 sets out the episode in full.

That is not a price cut. It is a quality-adjusted price increase presented as one, and it means the withdrawn $7,500 was absorbed through three channels rather than two: manufacturer margin, higher prices paid, and less product received for the same money. The third is invisible to any specification that regresses transaction price on subsidy without hedonic controls, and its omission will bias both estimated pass-through and any elasticity implied by the termination. Since at least one major manufacturer responded on the content margin within seven days, the bias is unlikely to be negligible.

The episode also speaks to the direction of incidence, and it points the same way as the discount evidence. A firm that had been capturing the subsidy as producer surplus would have had no need to restructure its product line when the subsidy vanished. Restructuring within a week is the behavior of a firm that had been passing the money through and suddenly had to find $7,500 of consumer-facing value somewhere else.

**Evidence pointing the other way, which should not be suppressed.** In February 2023, when Treasury reclassified certain crossovers as SUVs and thereby raised the applicable MSRP cap from $55,000 to $80,000, Tesla raised the price of the Model Y within days. The reclassification widened eligibility; the seller immediately captured part of the widening.

That is direct evidence of seller capture, and it sits uncomfortably beside the October 2025 evidence of pass-through. The two are reconcilable, and the reconciliation is instructive. The February 2023 change relaxed a *cap*, which mechanically created headroom between the old price and the new ceiling: a seller facing a binding constraint that is loosened will move into the space, and demand conditions in early 2023 were strong. The October 2025 change removed the *subsidy itself* under weak demand conditions. Incidence is not a constant of the market; it is a function of whether supply or demand is binding at the moment the policy moves, and it can run in opposite directions within the same program and the same firm inside three years.

The practical consequence for research is that any single-episode estimate of pass-through for this credit should be treated as local to its episode. The February 2023 reclassification and the September 2025 termination are two natural experiments on the same instrument with apparently opposite signs, and a study using only one will reach a confident and misleading conclusion. Chapter 9's research agenda should be read with that in mind.

## 6.3 Marginality: most recipients were inframarginal

The uncomfortable finding is that most people who received the credit would have bought the vehicle without it. Estimates reported in the Congressional Research Service's review of the economics of these credits put the inframarginal share at roughly 70%, drawing on the work of Xing, Leard and Li.

If 70% of recipients are inframarginal, then roughly $3.30 of subsidy is spent for every dollar that goes to a buyer whose behavior actually changed, before considering what the marginal buyer would otherwise have driven. That ratio is the standard critique of the credits, and it is a fair one on its own terms.

Two qualifications belong alongside it. First, inframarginality is measured against a counterfactual in which the credit does not exist for anyone; a program that sustains volume also sustains scale economies, charging investment, dealer familiarity, and used-vehicle supply, and those dynamic effects are not captured by a static marginality calculation. Second, and more directly relevant here, the marginal share should be expected to *rise* under point-of-sale delivery, because the buyers newly brought in by immediacy and effective refundability are precisely those who could not previously use the credit. The 70% figure comes from the pre-transfer era. Whether it fell in 2024 and 2025 is unknown and testable.

## 6.4 Distribution: the pre-2023 baseline and the 2023 correction

Borenstein and Davis, examining U.S. clean energy tax credits over their first decade, found that the top income quintile received roughly 90% of electric vehicle credit dollars, and about 60% of all clean energy credit dollars, while the bottom three quintiles together received about 10%. For the vehicle credit this is close to the most concentrated distributional profile a federal transfer program can have.

Two mechanisms produced it. Electric vehicles were expensive and were bought by the affluent. And the credit was non-refundable, so its value was mechanically proportional to tax liability over the relevant range, which means a household with modest liability received a fraction of the nominal amount and a household with none received nothing.

The Inflation Reduction Act attacked both mechanisms. The AGI caps and MSRP caps excluded the top of the distribution directly. Section 25E opened a channel into the used market. And the transfer election, combined with the rule that an advance payment is not recaptured for insufficient liability, defeated the non-refundability problem for anyone who elected.

The third of these is the most powerful and the least discussed. It is not an exaggeration to say that the transfer election did more for the distributional profile of the credit than the income caps did, because the income caps removed high-income recipients while the transfer election *added* low-liability ones. Removing the top of a distribution does not help the bottom; making the benefit usable at the bottom does.

No published study measures the resulting distribution for 2024 and 2025. The data exist: returns claiming §30D and §25E, with and without transfer elections, matched to AGI. This is the most valuable single analysis that could be run on the surviving administrative record, and Chapter 9 places it first on the research agenda.

## 6.5 The leasing channel

Now the largest leak.

Section 45W, the commercial clean vehicle credit, carried no AGI limits, no MSRP caps, no North American final assembly requirement, no critical minerals threshold, no battery components threshold, and no foreign-entity-of-concern exclusion. A leasing company that bought a vehicle and leased it to a consumer was the owner, the vehicle was business property, and the credit was available on those terms.

Consumers, and manufacturers, drew the obvious conclusion. A vehicle assembled outside North America, priced above the MSRP cap, sold to a household above the income limit, with a battery sourced entirely from excluded jurisdictions, was ineligible for a cent under §30D and eligible for up to $7,500 under §45W if the transaction was structured as a lease. Captive finance arms passed the credit through as capitalized cost reduction, marketed as lease cash. The share of new electric vehicles leased rose from roughly 12% in 2023 to roughly 35% in the first quarter of 2024. Manufacturers whose production sat outside North America, and who were therefore shut out of §30D, made the heaviest use of it.

Four consequences follow.

**The industrial policy was substantially nullified in the segment that used it.** Congress conditioned $3,750 of the credit on critical mineral sourcing and $3,750 on battery component manufacturing, escalating annually, to move supply chains. For roughly a third of new electric vehicle acquisitions, those conditions simply did not apply. Sourcing rules that a third of the market can opt out of by restructuring the contract are not sourcing rules; they are a tax on one financing form.

**The distributional rules were nullified with them.** The AGI and MSRP caps applied to purchases and not to leases. A household above the income limit buying a vehicle above the price cap received nothing through §30D and could receive up to $7,500 of lease cash through §45W. The 2022 reform's distributional program had a door in it.

**Pass-through was discretionary.** Nothing required a lessor to pass any part of the §45W credit to the lessee. Under §30D(g), the dealer had to pay the buyer the full credit as a condition of the transfer. Under §45W, the lessor kept whatever it chose. Reporting indicates that pass-through was common because lease markets are competitive, but "common" is not "required," and the variation across lessors and models is a natural experiment on pass-through that the industry data would support and that nobody has run.

**The paperwork asymmetry is stark.** The §30D and §25E buyer needed an accepted Form 15400 naming them, filed within three calendar days, or received nothing. The §45W lessor claimed on its own return with no counterparty reporting requirement of comparable rigidity. The strictest documentation burden in the regime fell on the retail buyer, and the loosest applied to the channel that escaped all of Congress's substantive conditions.

## 6.6 An assessment

Taken together, the 2022 reform bundled four programs with incompatible objectives into one section and then left open a channel that let a third of the market out of three of them. Adoption maximization argues for a large, simple, universal subsidy. Distributional targeting argues for income and price caps. Industrial policy argues for sourcing conditions that necessarily shrink the eligible set. Fiscal restraint argues for smaller amounts and tighter eligibility. These pull against each other, and the compromise made each program worse at its own objective than a clean version would have been, while §45W provided an escape hatch from the two that bind hardest.

The honest conclusion is not that the credits failed. Volumes rose, the used credit opened a channel that had never existed, and the transfer election made the benefit real for households that had never been able to use it. The conclusion is that the design paid for four objectives and bought a partial version of each, and that the leasing channel was a foreseeable and foreseen consequence of writing a business credit without the conditions attached to the consumer credit for the same vehicles.
