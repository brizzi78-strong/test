# Chapter 4. The transfer election as delivery-system redesign

## 4.1 What was elected

From January 1, 2024, a buyer of a qualifying new or previously-owned clean vehicle could elect to transfer the credit to the registered dealer at the time of sale. In exchange the dealer was required to pay the buyer the full amount of the allowable credit, in cash or as a reduction in the purchase price or down payment, no later than at the time of sale. The dealer then submitted the seller report and received an advance payment from Treasury, on a cycle reported as seventy-two hours.

Three parties, three transfers, one transaction. The buyer gives up a future tax credit and receives a present price reduction. The dealer gives up cash now and receives a Treasury payment in days. The government pays the same money it would otherwise have paid, several months earlier, to a different person, on the strength of an attestation rather than a return.

Everything interesting about the clean vehicle credits after 2024 follows from that restructuring.

## 4.2 Why the timing of a subsidy changes its value

Standard public finance treats a $7,500 credit received in April as worth $7,500 discounted by a few months of interest. The empirical literature does not support that treatment, for three reasons that compound in this setting.

**Liquidity constraints.** A household buying a $40,000 vehicle finances it. The size of the down payment determines the loan, and the loan determines the monthly payment, which is the variable most consumers actually optimize. A $7,500 reduction applied as capitalized cost reduction changes the monthly payment by roughly $130 to $150 on a five-year note. The same $7,500 arriving next April changes the monthly payment by nothing. For an unconstrained buyer the two are close substitutes; for a constrained buyer they are different products, and the constrained buyer is exactly the marginal buyer that adoption policy is trying to reach.

**Salience.** Chetty, Looney and Kroft showed that taxes not displayed at the register affect behavior less than taxes that are, and the result generalizes to subsidies. A discount printed on the buyer's order sheet and itemized on a government form is about as salient as a subsidy can be made. A line on a return prepared by someone else four months later is close to invisible.

**Uncertainty and complexity.** Before transfer, a buyer had to predict their own year-end tax liability to know what the credit was worth to them, and a non-trivial number would have discovered it was worth less than $7,500 or nothing. The take-up literature, including Bhargava and Manoli's work on the earned income tax credit, finds that complexity and uncertainty depress participation substantially even for large benefits among eligible populations who are informed of their eligibility. Transfer removed the prediction problem: the buyer received a known amount at a known time, and, because of the no-recapture-for-insufficient-liability rule, kept it.

The prediction is therefore unambiguous. Moving the credit to the point of sale should increase the behavioral response per subsidy dollar, and should do so most for lower-income buyers, for used vehicle buyers under §25E, and for buyers with low tax liability. Survey evidence on stated preferences over incentive form, including work by Roberson and Helveston, points the same way: immediate rebates are valued above nominally equivalent tax credits.

## 4.3 The dealer as fiscal intermediary

The transfer election made auto dealers into disbursing agents. This is not a metaphor. A registered dealer advanced federal money to a citizen on the government's behalf, certified the citizen's eligibility on a federal form, and was reimbursed from the Treasury. The functions are those of a fiscal intermediary, and the entity performing them was a private business with commercial interests in the same transaction.

The design took on three problems that come with intermediation.

**Selection into participation.** Roughly 12,200 sellers registered for the advance payment program, with another 3,400 registered for reporting only. The second number is the interesting one. Those sellers would file reports but not front the money, which means their customers could not obtain point-of-sale value. Participation in the advance payment program was voluntary, so the availability of the program's central benefit varied by dealership, and therefore geographically. No published analysis maps that variation. It is a natural instrument for identifying the effect of point-of-sale delivery on adoption, and Chapter 7 returns to it.

**Working capital.** A dealer advancing $7,500 per transaction and waiting days for reimbursement is extending short-term credit to the federal government. A high-volume store might carry six figures of exposure at any moment. Large dealer groups absorbed this easily. Small independent used-car dealers, the entire distribution channel for §25E, did not, and the credit aimed at the lowest-income buyers was administered by the least capitalized sellers. That mismatch deserves more study than it has received.

**Incentive misalignment on filing.** For a transferred credit, filing the report is how the dealer gets paid, so the incentive is strong. For a non-transferred credit, filing the report produces no dealer benefit whatsoever, imposes a three-day administrative burden, and the entire consequence of failure falls on the customer. The rational prediction is that non-transferred transactions would be under-reported relative to transferred ones. The 2025 filing season, described in Chapter 5, is consistent with that prediction, though the published accounts indicate failures in both categories.

## 4.4 Attestation as a verification technology

The buyer attests to income eligibility and to the intent to use the vehicle personally rather than for resale. The dealer attests to its own registration status and to the accuracy of the transaction data. Neither attestation is verified at the point of sale, because nothing can be verified at the point of sale; the AGI test is not determinable until the year closes.

This is a familiar structure: pay first on an attestation, verify later against the return, recapture from the person who attested falsely. It is the same architecture as advance premium tax credits under the Affordable Care Act, which reconcile on Form 8962, and it produces the same known pathology. Some fraction of recipients will receive an advance they are not entitled to keep and will face a repayment obligation at filing, typically without having set the money aside.

The clean vehicle regime softened the pathology in one direction and preserved it in the other. It waived recapture where the only problem was insufficient tax liability, which removed the largest and least culpable source of repayment shocks. It preserved recapture where the buyer's AGI exceeded the limit, which is the case where the buyer had information the government did not and attested anyway. That is a defensible allocation, and it is better designed than the ACA analogue.

## 4.5 What the redesign did not fix

Three problems survived the transfer election.

**The eligibility maze remained.** A buyer still had to know whether a specific trim of a specific model, assembled at a specific plant, with a battery of specific provenance, qualified for $7,500, $3,750, or nothing, under thresholds that escalated each January and foreign-entity exclusions that phased in across 2024 and 2025. Point-of-sale delivery makes the *payment* simple. It does nothing about eligibility complexity, and it arguably makes eligibility errors more consequential, because the money has already changed hands.

**The lease channel was untouched.** A consumer who leased received value only if the lessor chose to pass through its §45W credit, and nothing required pass-through. The transfer election guaranteed the buyer the full credit; the leasing channel guaranteed nothing. Chapter 6 takes this up.

**The buyer still bore the dealer's compliance risk.** This is the central failure, and the transfer election made it worse rather than better, because a buyer who took a $7,500 discount at the counter and later learned no report had been filed was in a materially more precarious position than a buyer who had merely lost a deduction on a return.

## 4.6 Summary

The transfer election is the most significant piece of tax-administration design in the Inflation Reduction Act's consumer provisions, and it worked in the sense that mattered: it made a deferred, liability-limited, invisible credit into an immediate, effectively refundable, conspicuous discount, and it did so without amending the credit's amount or its statutory character. The next chapter examines the cost of building that machine on a three-day deadline enforced against fifteen thousand small businesses, with the sanction pointed at their customers.
