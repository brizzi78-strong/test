# Chapter 5. Administrative fragility: the 2024–2025 reporting failures

## 5.1 What happened

The first year of the point-of-sale regime ran from January 1 to December 31, 2024. By the middle of December 2024 the IRS had processed more than 600,000 clean vehicle credit claims on returns filed during that calendar year, and had registered more than 12,200 sellers in the advance payment program plus roughly 3,400 more for reporting only.

Then the 2025 filing season began, and buyers started receiving notices telling them they could not claim the credit because the required time-of-sale report had never been successfully submitted by their dealer. The National Automobile Dealers Association reported hearing about the problem repeatedly, across both categories of transaction: cases where the dealer had applied the credit at the point of sale under a transfer election, and cases where the customer had intended to claim the credit on their own return.

The failure had several distinct causes, which the contemporaneous accounts do not always separate:

- Reports never filed at all, typically by dealers unaware of the obligation or unable to complete the portal workflow within three calendar days.
- Reports attempted but not successfully accepted, where the dealer believed it had filed and the submission had failed validation.
- Reports filed with defective data, most consequentially a wrong VIN or a wrong buyer taxpayer identification number, either of which breaks the match to the return.
- Reports for sales where the dealer had registered for reporting only, or had not completed registration, and therefore lacked authority to file.

From the buyer's perspective all four look identical: a purchase that satisfied every statutory condition, a credit that could not be claimed, and no available remedy, because the defect lay in a document the buyer could neither file nor compel.

## 5.2 The agency's response

Under pressure from dealers and their associations, the IRS reopened the reporting portal for 2024 transactions. NADA's March 25, 2025 alert reported that the portal would remain open indefinitely and would accept reporting for both traditional §30D claims and advance payment transactions. Professional commentary at the time treated the reopening as significant relief, particularly for dealers who had applied the credit at the point of sale and then lost the reimbursement because they had missed the filing window.

The reopening is the right decision and it should be read for what it concedes. A three-day statutory-adjacent deadline, enforced strictly, would have destroyed a large number of valid statutory entitlements for reasons unrelated to the merits. Faced with that, the agency effectively abandoned the deadline retrospectively. What it did not do, because it probably could not do it without legislation, was create a mechanism by which a buyer could establish eligibility when the dealer would not or could not file. Relief still ran through the dealer. A buyer whose dealer had gone out of business, or refused to engage, or could not be located, remained without recourse.

## 5.3 Risk allocation: an analysis

The right frame is the standard one for allocating the risk of a third party's failure: place it on the party best positioned to prevent it, to absorb it, or to insure against it.

**Prevention.** The dealer controls filing entirely. The buyer's only preventive act is to ask for the copy of Form 15400 before leaving, and even that does not confirm acceptance by the IRS if the copy was generated outside the portal or the submission later failed. On prevention, the case for placing the risk on the dealer is overwhelming.

**Absorption.** A $7,500 loss is material to almost any household and immaterial to almost any dealership. On absorption, the case for the dealer is again clear.

**Insurance.** Dealers carry errors-and-omissions coverage and can price compliance risk into operations. Households cannot insure against a counterparty's tax filing failure.

By all three criteria the risk was allocated to the wrong party. The reason is structural rather than deliberate. Section 30D(d)(1)(H) is drafted as a definitional attribute of the vehicle, so a missing report means there is no qualifying vehicle, and a taxpayer cannot claim a credit for a vehicle that does not qualify. The buyer's blamelessness is not a category the provision recognizes. Congress wrote a documentation requirement into the definition of the subsidized good, and the definitional drafting silently transferred the entire risk of seller non-compliance onto buyers.

## 5.4 Remedies that were available and were not used

Several mechanisms could have preserved the register's integrity without destroying blameless claims.

**A substitute-report procedure.** A buyer with a purchase agreement, a VIN, proof of payment, and a signed attestation could have been permitted to file a substitute report and put the burden on the IRS to reject it on the merits. The evidentiary record of a vehicle purchase is unusually strong. Registration and titling records establish that the sale occurred, to whom, and when. There is little scope here for the fabrication that a substitute-report procedure invites in less documented contexts.

**Penalties on the dealer instead of forfeiture by the buyer.** The Code's ordinary approach to information-return failures is a penalty on the filer under §§6721 and 6722, not the destruction of the payee's substantive rights. That approach was available and was not chosen.

**A confirmation obligation running to the buyer.** The system could have sent the buyer an independent acknowledgement, by mail or through an online account, confirming that an accepted report naming them existed. The buyer would then have known within days, when the dealer was still reachable and the transaction fresh, rather than in April of the following year. The infrastructure for this exists; individual online accounts already display far more sensitive information.

**Business-day rather than calendar-day counting.** A three-business-day rule would have absorbed the weekend problem that is inherent to retail automotive sales at essentially no cost to register integrity.

None of these is technically difficult. The first two require statutory change; the second two do not.

## 5.5 The scale of the problem, and what is not known

Here the analysis runs into the limits of the public record, and it is worth stating the limits precisely rather than gesturing at them.

What is documented: more than 600,000 claims processed on 2024-filed returns; about 15,600 registered sellers across both categories; a pattern of failures reported by dealer associations and tax practitioners in the 2025 filing season; and an indefinite reopening of the portal for 2024 transactions.

What is not published, and what any serious evaluation requires: the number of sales for which no report was ever filed; the number of buyers who claimed and were denied; the number who never attempted to claim after being told they could not; the distribution of failures across dealer size, geography, transferred versus non-transferred transactions, and new versus used; and the number of claims cured by the reopened portal. The IRS holds all of it. None of it has been released in a form researchers can use.

The most policy-relevant of these is the failure rate by transaction type, because it tests the incentive-misalignment prediction of Chapter 4 directly. If non-transferred sales failed at a materially higher rate than transferred sales, the mechanism is confirmed: dealers filed reliably when their own reimbursement depended on it and unreliably when only the customer's credit was at stake. That is a finding with immediate design implications for any future point-of-sale program, and it is one query against data the government already possesses.

The practical question this leaves for an affected buyer, namely what can actually be done when a dealership will not or cannot file, is taken up separately in the practitioner annex at Chapter 10.

## 5.6 The used-vehicle case

Section 25E deserves separate treatment because its structural fragility was worse on every dimension.

The maximum credit was $4,000 against a purchase price of no more than $25,000, so the credit was a much larger fraction of the transaction than under §30D and the buyers were, by construction, lower on the income distribution. Those buyers are the most likely to be liquidity-constrained, the most likely to have insufficient tax liability to use a non-refundable credit, and therefore the most dependent on point-of-sale transfer. They are also the least equipped to absorb a $4,000 loss and the least likely to have a tax preparer who will fight about it.

The sellers, meanwhile, were independent used-car dealers: small, numerous, thinly capitalized, high-turnover in staffing, and without the compliance departments that franchised new-car stores maintain. They had to register in ECO, understand an unfamiliar credit, front $4,000 per transaction, and file within three days.

The result was a benefit whose delivery depended on the compliance capacity of the least capable link in the entire retail automotive chain, aimed at the buyers least able to bear its failure. Whether §25E in fact failed at a higher rate is unknown for the same reason as everything else in this chapter. The design predicts it, and the prediction should be tested before anyone builds another point-of-sale credit on the same foundation.

## 5.7 Summary

The 2024–2025 reporting failures were not an implementation accident to be dismissed as first-year turbulence. They were the direct consequence of two design choices: making a third party's filing a definitional condition of the subsidized good, and providing no independent path for the beneficiary to establish eligibility. The agency mitigated the consequences retrospectively and generously. The underlying allocation of risk was never corrected, and the credits were terminated before it could be.
