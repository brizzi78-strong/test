# Front Matter

## Abstract

Between January 2024 and September 2025 the United States ran, through its income tax code, something close to a national point-of-sale rebate program for electric vehicles. It was not described that way in the statute. Internal Revenue Code §30D remained a non-refundable personal credit, §25E a smaller one for used vehicles, and §45W a business credit. What made the program function as a rebate was a transfer election, an online registration system for dealers, and a single document: Form 15400, the Clean Vehicle Seller Report, filed to IRS Energy Credits Online within three calendar days of delivery and given to the buyer as a receipt.

This dissertation takes that form as its object of study. It argues that the administrative architecture assembled around Form 15400, rather than the size or the eligibility rules of the credits, explains most of what is interesting about the 2023–2026 period: the sharp rise in take-up and in the share of the subsidy reaching liquidity-constrained buyers; the emergence of a novel failure mode in which a dealer's clerical omission destroyed a buyer's statutory entitlement; the displacement of a large fraction of the market into the §45W leasing channel, which carried none of the sourcing, price, or income conditions Congress had attached to §30D; and the political vulnerability of a subsidy that had been made conspicuous. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, Pub. L. No. 119-21 (July 4, 2025), terminated §30D, §25E, and §45W for vehicles acquired after September 30, 2025.

The dissertation then treats the termination as a natural experiment. Battery-electric vehicles reached a record 11.3% of U.S. new-vehicle sales in September 2025 and fell to 5.9% in October. First-half 2026 volumes were roughly 463,000 units, down about 24% year over year, with a partial quarter-over-quarter recovery in Q2 2026 as manufacturer discounts absorbed part of the withdrawn subsidy. The pull-forward and the crash are separable from the level effect only because the transfer election had already made the credit's timing and magnitude visible to buyers. The methodological payoff is real, and the chapter sets out the designs that the surviving administrative data would support.

The concluding argument is general. Form 15400 is a small instance of a large move in fiscal administration: delegating the verification and delivery of a public benefit to a commercial intermediary that already sits at the transaction. That move buys take-up, salience, and immediacy. It sells off the state's direct relationship with the beneficiary, and with it the ability to guarantee that a person who satisfies the statute actually receives what the statute grants. Any future point-of-sale credit, in energy or elsewhere, has to decide who bears the risk of the intermediary's failure. The clean vehicle credits decided it in favor of the government and against the buyer, and never revisited the choice.

## Thesis statement

The clean vehicle credits' turn to point-of-sale transfer, effected through Form 15400 and IRS Energy Credits Online, converted a tax expenditure into a rebate administered by auto dealers. This raised take-up and salience, relocated the program's failure risk onto buyers who depended on third-party compliance they could not control, and made the subsidy politically legible enough to repeal cleanly. The design and the demise are the same fact.

## Research questions

1. What, precisely, did Form 15400 do, and what did the transfer election require of the parties around it?
2. Did the shift to point-of-sale delivery change who received the credit, and how much of it they kept?
3. Where did the risk of administrative failure fall, and why did it fall there?
4. How much of the §30D architecture, and of Congress's industrial-policy conditions, was undone by the §45W leasing channel?
5. What can be identified from the September 30, 2025 termination, and with what data?
6. What should the design of a future point-of-sale credit look like?

## Methods and source note

This is a documentary and interpretive study with a quantitative chapter. It works from four bodies of material: the statutes and their amending acts; published administrative guidance, principally Rev. Proc. 2022-42, Rev. Proc. 2023-33, the §30D regulations finalized in 2024, and the IRS frequently-asked-questions issued after the One Big Beautiful Bill Act; contemporaneous trade and professional reporting from dealer associations, accounting firms, and the automotive press; and the empirical economics literature on subsidy incidence, take-up, and salience.

Two limitations should be stated at the outset rather than buried.

First, the research environment for this document had restricted access to primary-source websites. Statutory and regulatory content, IRS guidance language, and market statistics were obtained through search summaries and secondary reporting rather than by retrieving the underlying documents in full. Every factual claim in this dissertation that depends on such a source is marked in the text and catalogued in Appendix E. Section numbers, revenue procedure numbers, dates, and dollar figures should be verified against the primary sources before any of this is relied upon in practice or in a submitted academic version.

Second, market data for late 2025 and 2026 come from industry sources (Cox Automotive, NADA, Edmunds, Experian) whose series are constructed differently. Where two figures for the same month disagree, both are reported and the discrepancy is discussed rather than resolved by choosing the more convenient number. Chapter 7 is explicit about which comparisons the data can and cannot support.

The dissertation does not attempt original econometric estimation. It specifies designs and identifies the data that would be needed to execute them, which is the honest limit of what can be done without access to the administrative microdata.

## Statement on scope

"Clean energy credit" in the title of this project refers to the clean *vehicle* credits and their administration. The residential and commercial energy credits (§25C, §25D, §45L, §179D) and the technology-neutral electricity credits (§45Y, §48E) enter only in Chapter 8, where they matter as the next candidates for the delivery-system design questions raised here. The charging-equipment credit, §30C, appears where it bears on the vehicle credits' effect.
