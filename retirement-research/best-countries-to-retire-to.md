# Best Countries to Retire To

A practical guide to where your retirement income goes furthest, current as of September 2026.

## What actually matters when you're choosing

Most "best places to retire" lists lead with beaches and sunsets. Those matter, but they're not what determines whether you're still happy there in year three. The things that actually decide it:

- **Cost of living relative to your income.** Not just rent — groceries, transportation, and the small daily spending that adds up faster than people expect.
- **Healthcare you can actually use.** Is there a public system you can join as a resident, and is private coverage affordable if you want to skip the wait times?
- **A visa path that doesn't expire on you.** A tourist stamp is not a retirement plan. You want a residency category built for retirees, with income requirements you can actually clear.
- **Tax treatment of foreign pensions and retirement accounts.** A cheap country that taxes your Social Security or 401(k) withdrawals at a high rate isn't actually cheap.
- **Safety, climate, and how easy it is to build a life** — medical Spanish or Portuguese you can get by with, an expat community if you want one, direct flights home.

Below are eight countries that consistently clear all five, with the real numbers behind each one — plus four under-the-radar alternatives further down for anyone tired of competing with other retirees for the same three neighborhoods.

## Portugal

Portugal has been the default answer to "where should I retire" for years now, and it still earns it. The **D7 visa** — built for passive income like pensions, dividends, or rental income — requires proof of roughly **€920/month** (about €11,040/year) as of 2026, the lowest income bar in Western Europe. That figure rises 50% for a spouse and 30% per dependent child.

The catch: the old NHR tax regime, which let new residents shelter foreign pension income from Portuguese tax for a decade, is gone. It was replaced in 2025 by **IFICI ("NHR 2.0")**, which is narrower — aimed at scientific researchers and innovation-sector professionals — and no longer covers pension income the way NHR did. Retirees moving to Portugal today should plan around ordinary Portuguese tax rates on foreign pensions, not the old exemption, and get a cross-border tax consultation before committing.

What you get in return: the **Serviço Nacional de Saúde**, a public healthcare system that's genuinely good and cheap once you're a legal resident, safety levels most Americans aren't used to, and a huge, established expat network in Lisbon, Porto, and the Algarve. Rent outside the big cities still runs well below Western European norms, though Lisbon itself has gotten expensive.

## Panama

Panama's **Pensionado visa** is the most retiree-friendly program in the hemisphere. You need a lifetime pension of **$1,000/month** from a government, employer, or qualifying private annuity — and that drops to **$750/month** if you also buy Panamanian real estate worth over $100,000. Social Security, military pensions, and most private annuities qualify.

The Pensionado program comes with real, legislated discounts baked into law — on everything from medical visits to movie tickets to airfare — not just marketing copy. Panama uses the US dollar, which removes currency risk entirely for American retirees, and Panama City has private hospitals that meet US standards at a fraction of US prices. It's also one of the few countries here with no age minimum for the visa, so it works for people retiring early.

## Mexico

Mexico remains the closest, cheapest, and most logistically simple option for North Americans, but the numbers moved in 2026. Consulates switched from calculating income thresholds off minimum wage to the UMA (a Mexican economic index), and the effective bar for a **temporary resident visa** now sits around **$4,300–$4,500/month** in income over the trailing six months — or a **$70,000** average bank/investment balance over twelve months. That's a meaningfully higher bar than it was a few years ago, so check current consulate figures before assuming you qualify.

Once you clear it, Mexico delivers: colonial cities like San Miguel de Allende and Mérida with huge, mature expat communities; a one-hour flight from much of the US; and a genuinely good private healthcare system in the major cities, priced at a fraction of US costs. IMSS, the public system, is also open to residents for a modest annual fee. The tradeoff for the low cost is that Mexico's northern border regions have real security concerns — most retirees settle well south of them, in the interior or the Yucatán, where the picture is very different.

## Costa Rica

Costa Rica's **Pensionado visa** requires a pension of **$1,000/month from a single source** — Social Security or a private pension — and you can't combine a spouse's income to hit the number; each qualifying pensioner needs their own. The visa (and your residency card, the DIMEX) renews every two years, and you're required to enroll in **CAJA**, the national healthcare system, which is inexpensive and covers residents regardless of pre-existing conditions.

What you're paying for is stability: Costa Rica abolished its military in 1948 and has spent the decades since as one of the more politically settled countries in Latin America, with a strong environmental record and a genuinely happy population (it tops regional well-being indexes most years). It's not the cheapest option on this list — the Central Valley and the popular coastal towns have gotten pricier as more retirees have moved in — but the CAJA system and the stability are worth the premium for a lot of people.

## Ecuador

If budget is the deciding factor, Ecuador is hard to beat among countries with a real retiree visa. The **pensioner visa** requires only **$800/month** in verifiable pension income, and cities like **Cuenca** — a UNESCO World Heritage colonial city at altitude with spring-like weather year-round — let people live comfortably on **$1,500/month or less**, including rent.

Ecuador also uses the US dollar, so there's no currency conversion to think about, and residents can join **IESS**, the public health system, for a low monthly premium. The coastal towns (Salinas, Manta) offer a different, beachier version of the same low-cost equation. The main adjustments: altitude in Cuenca (about 8,200 feet) takes some people a few weeks to acclimate to, and Spanish fluency matters more here than in more heavily English-adapted expat hubs.

## Spain

Spain doesn't have a dedicated pensionado visa the way Panama or Costa Rica do — retirees typically use the **non-lucrative visa**, which requires proving sufficient passive income (roughly 400% of Spain's IPREM index, which works out to somewhere in the $30,000+/year range for a single applicant) and explicitly bars you from working locally. It's a higher bar than Portugal's D7, and Spain taxes worldwide income for residents with no NHR-style shelter, so the tax planning matters even more here.

What you get for clearing it: France-adjacent healthcare quality, walkable cities, a genuinely temperate climate on the southern coasts, and a lifestyle built around long meals and long lives — Spain has one of the highest life expectancies in the world. Retirees typically budget **$2,500–$3,500/month** for a comfortable life outside Madrid and Barcelona, more in those two cities.

## Thailand

Thailand's **retirement visa (O-A/O-X)** is available to anyone 50 or older who can show roughly **800,000 baht** (about $22,000–$24,000) in a Thai bank account, or an equivalent monthly income, or a combination of both. It's a bureaucratic process with annual renewals and 90-day address reporting, but tens of thousands of Western retirees navigate it every year, concentrated in Chiang Mai, Hua Hin, and parts of Bangkok.

The draw is the cost-to-quality ratio: private hospital care in Bangkok is internationally accredited and dramatically cheaper than US equivalents, a couple can live well on **$1,500–$2,000/month** outside the most touristy areas, and the climate, food, and infrastructure are hard to match at that price point. The tradeoff is distance — this is not a place you fly home from for a weekend — and a visa system that rewards people willing to deal with paperwork every year.

## Greece

Greece is the newest entrant to the top of these rankings, and for 2026 it actually took the top overall spot in one of the major retirement indexes for the first time. The pitch is straightforward: **300+ days of sun a year**, a Mediterranean lifestyle at roughly a third of the cost of comparable US or Northern European living, EU healthcare access once you're a legal resident, and a **Financially Independent Person visa** with income requirements that are modest by Western European standards.

It's less established as an expat destination than Portugal or Spain, which cuts both ways — fewer English-speaking support networks, but also less of the price inflation that heavy expat demand has brought to the Algarve or Lisbon. Athens, Thessaloniki, and the islands each offer a genuinely different version of retirement, from full city amenities to slower island life.

## Under the radar: destinations without the crowds

The eight above are the safe answers — well-trodden, well-documented, and increasingly full of other retirees driving up rents in the same three neighborhoods. If you want the same math (cheap living, workable visa, tax-friendly treatment of pensions) without the crowds, four places are quietly running better numbers than almost anywhere on the mainstream list.

**Albania.** The **Residence Permit for Retired Persons** requires roughly **€900–967/month** in pension income — in the same range as Portugal's D7, with none of Portugal's competition for it. Foreign-sourced pension income is taxed at **0%** for qualifying retirees, a real advantage over Portugal's post-NHR regime. A couple lives comfortably in Tirana on **€1,500–2,000/month**, less on the Albanian Riviera in Saranda or Vlora — a coastline frequently compared to Croatia's at a third of the price, largely because Albania spent decades closed off and is only now showing up on retirement lists. Private health coverage (roughly €75–230/month depending on age) is required alongside the permit, and this route doesn't lead to citizenship — it's a long-term legal-residence status, not a path to a passport, which is a fair tradeoff for most people just looking for an affordable base.

**Georgia** (the country, not the state). This is the most accessible option on this entire list: most nationalities get **365 days visa-free with just a passport** — no application, no income proof, nothing to file before you arrive. If you want to formalize residency beyond that, a temporary residence permit needs only about **1,800 GEL/month** (~$650) in documented passive income. Tbilisi is walkable, genuinely cheap — a single retiree lives well on **$1,600–1,800/month**, a couple under $2,000 — and foreign pensions are untaxed under Georgia's territorial tax system. The catch is infrastructure and healthcare quality: it's improving fast but isn't at Western European levels yet, and mandatory health insurance (minimum ~$11,000 in coverage) is required for any stay past 30 days.

**Uruguay.** This is the "under the radar" option for people who actually want more structure, not less. The **pensionado benefit** requires **$1,500/month** for a single applicant or **$2,500/month** for a couple, plus a **$100,000 investment** (property or government bonds held ten years) — but in exchange you get **permanent residency on the first application** with no annual renewals, a route to citizenship, and one of the most politically stable, low-corruption countries in the hemisphere. It's not cheap by regional standards — plan on **$1,600–2,300+/month** for a single person, more for a family — but you're paying for Switzerland-of-South-America stability rather than rock-bottom prices. Note that a 2026 tax law change (Law 20.446) tightened the foreign-income tax holiday, so get current tax advice before assuming the old low-tax pitch still applies.

**Malaysia.** MM2H (Malaysia My Second Home) isn't cheap to enter — the entry-level **Silver tier** needs a **$150,000 fixed deposit** plus a property purchase of at least RM 600,000, though a newer **SEZ tier** drops that to **$32,000** for applicants 50 and older — but it buys a 5-to-20-year visa (depending on tier) in a country most Western retirees never seriously consider despite genuinely modern infrastructure, English widely spoken, excellent and cheap private healthcare, and a monthly budget around **$1,500** covering a comfortable life in Penang or Kuala Lumpur. It sits outside the usual Latin America/Southern Europe conversation entirely, which is exactly why it's underpriced relative to what it offers.

The common thread: each of these asks you to trade something the mainstream list doesn't — Albania trades name recognition and a path to citizenship, Georgia trades healthcare infrastructure, Uruguay trades cheapness for stability, Malaysia trades geographic familiarity. None of that is a downgrade, just a different bet than the Portugal-Panama-Mexico consensus everyone else is chasing.

## Case study: Portugal as home base, Sicily as somewhere you travel to

This is the simplest and most common version of the "second country" instinct: Portugal is home — where the D7 residency lives, where the address and the routine are — and Sicily is somewhere you go and come back from, not a second home base. Treated that way, it's close to friction-free.

**How much you can travel without complications.** As a legal resident of Portugal, a Schengen country, you can spend up to **90 days in any rolling 180-day period** in the rest of the Schengen area combined — Sicily included — with no separate visa, no application, nothing to arrange in advance. That's a real season: a full spring in the Val di Noto, or two shorter trips spread across the year. For a home-base-and-travel pattern, that ceiling is rarely the thing that binds. Since April 2026 the EU's Entry/Exit System tracks the count automatically at the border, so you always know where you stand without doing the math yourself.

**Portugal stays home no matter how much you travel, within reason.** Your D7 permit has its own separate limit — you can't be away from Portugal more than **six consecutive months**, and after renewal, no more than **eight months total** over a three-year permit — but ordinary travel, including a long Sicily stay, sits nowhere near that ceiling. It only becomes worth tracking if Sicily trips start stacking back-to-back with other long absences in the same year.

**The one thing worth knowing, not managing.** If a Sicily stay ever crept toward half the year — well beyond travel and into effectively living there — it could theoretically bump into Italy's own tax-residency test (183 days, or a "center of vital interests" test) independent of the Schengen visa count. For a visit-and-return pattern this is a ceiling to be aware of, not a risk to actively track.

**Why Sicily specifically is worth the trip.** It's dramatically cheap even by Southern European standards: rural inland towns run **€700–1,100/month** for a full cost of living, Palermo and Catania rent for **€400–700/month**, and UNESCO Baroque towns like Ragusa, Modica, and Noto rent for **€300–500/month** — all well under Algarve prices. Property is even more striking: habitable apartments in the Val di Noto start under **€50,000**, and several interior towns still run "**€1 house**" schemes for buyers who commit to renovating within a year. None of that requires Italian residency — you can own property in Italy as a non-resident with no restriction, which makes a Sicily pied-à-terre a straightforward way to have a place to return to.

**If it ever became more than travel.** Should Sicily someday pull more than a season's worth of time, Italy has its own answer to Portugal's old NHR: a **7% flat tax on foreign pension income** for retirees who actually move their tax residency to a qualifying small municipality (population under 20,000) in the south, Sicily included, for up to nine years. That's a different plan entirely — becoming an Italian resident instead of a Portuguese one — not something that applies to a travel pattern, but worth knowing it exists if the balance ever shifted.

## How to actually decide

Run the numbers against your real retirement income, not an average. A $1,000/month pension clears Panama's or Costa Rica's bar comfortably but won't touch Spain's non-lucrative visa threshold. Then weight healthcare and stability by your own health situation and risk tolerance — a public system you can join cheaply (Portugal, Costa Rica, Ecuador) matters more the older you are or the more pre-existing conditions you're managing.

Before committing to any of these, three things are worth doing regardless of destination:

1. **Get a cross-border tax consultation**, not a general one. How your specific pension, Social Security, or retirement account is taxed varies enormously by country and by tax treaty, and the rules shift often enough — see Portugal's NHR replacement — that year-old blog posts can be wrong.
2. **Rent before you buy.** Spend at least one full off-season in a place before committing capital. The version of a coastal town in July is not the version you'll live in the rest of the year.
3. **Verify current visa numbers directly with the consulate or embassy**, not a secondhand guide. Every income threshold in this document is accurate as of September 2026, and every one of these countries has changed its numbers at least once in the last few years.
