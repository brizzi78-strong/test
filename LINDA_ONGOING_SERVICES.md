# Positioning Linda: ongoing service without breaking the model

The model's promise is *communities pay our fee, families never do.* Linda's
RN is the agency's most credible asset, and the question is how it becomes an
ongoing, recurring line without turning that promise into fine print. The
answer is a bright line in time: **everything before move-in stays free and
community-paid; RN advocacy after move-in is optional and family-paid.**
Before move-in there is a steering incentive to protect against; after the
fee is earned, there isn't.

## 1. The RN Care Advocate — the recurring line

**What it is.** A post-placement subscription, sold at the 30-day check-in
(which is already promised free). Linda becomes the family's nurse in the
building:

| Included | Why families pay for it |
| --- | --- |
| Monthly visit, with a written update to the family | Out-of-town adult children — "kids in Ohio" — cannot see for themselves |
| Attendance at care-plan conferences | The meeting where the level of care (and the bill) gets set; nobody in the family knows what to ask |
| Medication and care-level review with the community's nurse | An RN reading the MAR catches what a daughter can't |
| Hospitalization navigation: ER, discharge back to the community, or not | The moment memory care families fall apart, and the moment the community may quietly decline readmission |
| Hospice and end-of-life planning conversations, when the time comes | The founder's whole reason for being — presence at the end |

**Price.** $300–450/month, or ~$150/hour for one-off help. Ten advocate
families is $36–54K/year of recurring revenue on Linda's time, with no
community dependency and no cash-flow gap.

**Who buys it.** Long-distance adult children; families placing into memory
care (highest anxiety, highest acuity, most hospitalizations); families the
elder law attorneys and care managers refer (they see the need before we do).

**Guardrails — these make it defensible:**
- Offered only *after* placement. Never a paid pre-placement service.
- **No placement fee on any re-placement of an advocate family.** If Linda
  concludes a client needs to move, the company earns nothing on the move.
  That removes the one incentive a skeptic would point to, and it's the
  sentence that lets care managers refer to her.
- Advocacy and care coordination, not hands-on nursing. She observes,
  reviews, advises, attends, and escalates. No treatments, no orders. That
  keeps the company outside home care licensure and keeps the site's "we do
  not assess patients or write care plans" line true in spirit. Professional
  liability (E&O) with RN advocacy explicitly covered; ask the broker.
- ALCA membership is optional and probably not worth it — its ethics code
  bars provider referral fees, which the placement side takes. Linda's title
  is *RN Care Advocate*, not Aging Life Care Professional, and that's fine:
  families buy the RN, not the acronym.

## 2. "Care manager" — the positioning

Don't call her a geriatric care manager. That name belongs to the ALCA
profession, whose members cannot take provider fees and who are your best
second-ring referrers — competing with them under their own label costs you
the channel. Position her as **the nurse who stays after the move**, which is
a gap care managers mostly don't fill (they're pre-crisis and whole-life; she's
post-placement and building-specific). Care managers will refer *to* her for
exactly that.

## 3. The FL-2 — Linda's fastest way to be indispensable, at no charge

North Carolina requires an **FL-2 (Level of Care Screening)** completed by a
physician, NP, or PA before admission to an adult care home or nursing
facility. Hospitals produce it on discharge; families moving from home,
independent living, or a private duty arrangement have to get it from a
primary care office, and that delays move-ins by days to weeks. Common snags:
the form says "nursing facility" when the family wants assisted living; the
dementia diagnosis needed for a special care unit is missing; the PCP's
office sits on it.

**Linda's role, included in placement (never billed to the family):**
- Pre-fill from records, coordinate with the PCP office, and read the form
  before it's submitted so it reflects the right level of care and diagnoses.
- Turn "we'll get it done sometime" into "it's done Thursday."

**The partner synergy.** The private duty company is physician-owned. For
families who are his patients, or whom his practice can legitimately see
(house call or telehealth, billed as a visit), the FL-2 can be completed in
days rather than weeks. That is a lawful clinical service he bills for — not
a referral payment — and it gives the pilot a concrete operational advantage
to measure. Keep the two things separate on paper: his practice evaluates and
signs; the agency places; neither pays the other for either.

**Why it matters commercially.** Move-in speed is the agency's leverage with
communities ("we fill a bed in days") and with planners. An RN who makes the
FL-2 disappear as a problem is a capability no call center can claim.

## 4. Move-in help — partner, don't staff

The physical move — packing, movers, furniture placement, utilities, mail,
the hundred small tasks — is a profession: **senior move managers** (the
NASMM directory lists them by area). Two options:

| Option | Verdict |
| --- | --- |
| Refer to a senior move manager; they may pay a bird-dog fee (non-clinical, so it's clean) | **Do this now.** Zero payroll, and it's one more reciprocal referral relationship |
| Build a paid move-in service in-house | Later, if ever. It's a labor business with trucks and workers' comp |

Cardinal's included move-in role stays what the site already promises:
coordination and presence on the day — not labor.

## 5. The podcast — Linda hosts, and every guest is a referrer

A podcast solves two problems at once: the founder can't be the public face,
and the referral network needs a reason to talk to you.

- **Host:** Linda. RN, co-founder, already public on the site.
- **Name:** *It's Not Your Fault* — the book's title, already the message.
- **Format:** 15–20 minutes, one question per episode, one guest.
- **The move:** every guest is a referrer — an elder law attorney on paying
  for memory care, a care manager on when it's time, a memory clinic social
  worker on the diagnosis conversation, a sales director on what a tour
  should show you, a senior move manager on the day itself, the private duty
  owner on the "money's getting tight" conversation. Each guest shares their
  episode with their clients. The podcast is a referral-network-building
  device that happens to produce content.
- **Production:** record on a phone or laptop; the Descript connection this
  workspace already has does the editing (filler-word removal, captions,
  clips for social). Two hours a month.
- **First eight episodes:** When is it time for memory care? · What memory
  care actually costs in Raleigh · The FL-2, explained · How to pay for it
  (attorney) · What to look for on a tour · The move-in day · When Mom
  refuses · Hospice is not giving up.

## What this adds up to

Before move-in: free, community-paid, RN-informed, FL-2-fast. After move-in:
an optional RN advocate the family pays for, with no fee on re-placements.
Around it: a podcast that interviews the referral network into existence.
Linda's RN is doing work in all three, and none of it contradicts the line on
the front of the website.
