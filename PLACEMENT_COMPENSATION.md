# Paying people to place — commission structures

Companion to [`PLACEMENT_GROWTH_PLAN.md`](PLACEMENT_GROWTH_PLAN.md). Covers the
four ways someone else can earn off a placement, what each is worth, and the one
category of person you must never put on a per-referral payment.

Planning number throughout: **$4,500 collected fee per placement** (Triangle
mix). Adjust down ~40–50% for the Florida panhandle, where assisted living runs
$2,295–3,969/mo against NC's ~$6,350.

---

## Rule zero: pay on collected, never on move-in

The community pays you net 30–60 **after** move-in, and can claw back if the
resident leaves inside the window. If you pay your person at move-in, you are
fronting your own cash and eating every clawback alone.

```
move-in ──▶ you invoice ──▶ community pays ──▶ you pay him
  wk 4-8        wk 8           wk 12-16         wk 12-16
```

Say it in the agreement in one sentence: *"Commission is earned when the
referral fee is collected, and is refundable to the company if the community
reverses the fee."* Everything else is negotiable. This isn't.

## Four roles, four rates

The rate follows **who owns the family** and **whose assets get used**. Most
disputes come from paying an advisor rate to someone doing bird-dog work, or
vice versa.

| Role | What they actually do | Rate | Per placement @ $4,500 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Bird dog** | Hands you a name and a phone number. You do everything else. | 10% or flat **$400–500** | ~$450 |
| **Part-time advisor** | Owns the family inquiry → move-in, using *your* community agreements, brand, and intake | **40–50%** | $1,800–2,250 |
| **Full-time advisor (W-2)** | Same, plus you feed them leads and pay a base | **20–30%** + base | $900–1,350 + base |
| **Out-of-market affiliate (1099)** | Builds their own supply *and* demand in a new metro from zero | **60–70%** | market-dependent |

The full-timer's rate looks low next to the part-timer's. It isn't — they're
getting a salary, your leads, and your calendar. The part-timer is getting none
of that, which is exactly why they get more of the fee.

## The side guy, specifically

Someone who wants to do this "on the side" is almost always describing role 1
while picturing role 2. Sort that out in the first conversation.

**Start him as a bird dog. Flat $500 per closed placement.**

Why flat and not a percentage:
- He can explain it to himself in one sentence. "$500 when they move in and the
  community pays."
- It doesn't require showing him your fee schedule, which is the one piece of
  commercial information you don't want circulating.
- It's a real number for a part-timer — three placements a quarter is $1,500 for
  work he did around a day job.

**Then graduate him — or don't.** After 3 closed placements, if he wants more
money and has shown judgment with families, move him to **40% as a part-time
advisor**, with a 90-day trial and the option to go back. If he doesn't want
more responsibility, leave him at $500 forever. A steady bird dog at $500 a
head is a good deal for both of you and requires nothing from you but a phone.

**Zero base. Zero draw.** A person with a day job does not need a draw, and a
draw is the mechanism by which you lose real money on someone who was never
going to produce. If he asks for one, he's asking to be an employee — different
conversation, different rate, and probably not yet.

### What actually makes a good side guy

You're not looking for a salesperson. You're looking for someone who already
stands where frightened families stand:

- home health / hospice liaisons and marketers
- SNF admissions or activities staff *(read the legal section first)*
- church care-ministry leads, parish nurses, chaplains
- senior move managers, downsizing realtors, estate-sale operators
- elder-law paralegals, fiduciaries, professional guardians

The bad profile is a general salesperson with no access to the population. He'll
be enthusiastic, produce nothing, and cost you three months of attention.

### The risk isn't the money

$500 a placement is cheap. What's expensive is that he's talking to a family
about their mother, wearing your name. One pushy conversation on a hospital
floor and a discharge planner quietly stops calling you — and they will never
tell you why. That's the real argument for starting him at bird dog: **he can't
damage a relationship he isn't in the room for.**

Before he ever tours a family or opines on care level, you sit in on two of his
calls. Non-negotiable.

## The line you cannot cross

**Do not pay a per-referral fee to anyone employed by a hospital, SNF, home
health agency, or hospice for referrals arising out of that job.**

This is the naive version of "pay a guy on the side," and it's the one that ends
badly. Paying a discharge planner or case manager per referral is, at absolute
minimum, a fireable offense for them and the permanent end of that facility as
a channel for you. Where any federal healthcare program dollars touch the
episode, it can also implicate the **federal Anti-Kickback Statute** — which is
criminal, and which applies to the payer as well as the payee.

Private-pay assisted living placement generally sits outside Medicare and
Medicaid billing, which is why the referral-fee model exists at all. But
"generally" is not a defense strategy, and the facts of a specific arrangement
are what matter. **Verify with a healthcare attorney before paying any clinical
or discharge-side employee anything.** Flagged, not resolved.

The safe version of that relationship is the one the whole plan is built on:
you earn the discharge planner's referrals by being fast and being right. Free
is not a weaker offer than $500 here — it's the only offer that doesn't destroy
the channel.

## Mechanics to put in writing

**Registered lead.** He submits the family's name in writing *before* he works
it — text or a form, timestamped. No registration, no commission. This kills
the "that was my family" argument before it happens, and stops him claiming a
lead that came in through your website.

**Attribution window.** His claim on a registered lead runs 6–12 months. A
family that tours in March and moves in July is still his.

**Clawback pass-through.** If the community reverses the fee, he refunds it or
it nets against his next payout. Spell out which — netting is easier to collect.

**Non-solicit, not non-compete.** He can't take your signed community
agreements or your referral contacts with him. Don't try to stop him from
working in senior care generally; NC courts are unfriendly to broad
non-competes, and you'd rather have the enforceable narrow clause.

**1099, not W-2.** No set hours, no required schedule, his own phone and car,
paid per outcome. A genuine side guy is the cleanest 1099 case there is — but
the classification follows the *facts*, not the paperwork. The moment you start
setting his schedule and dictating his methods, he's an employee in the eyes of
the IRS and NC DOL regardless of what the contract says. File the 1099-NEC.

**Fee disclosure to families.** Whatever you pay him, the family still hears the
same line from whoever is in front of them: *communities pay our fee, never
you.* If he can't deliver that comfortably and unprompted, he isn't ready to
talk to families.

| Question | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| Side guy, sends you names only | **$400–500 flat** per collected placement |

| Base or draw | **No** |
| Employment type | 1099 |
