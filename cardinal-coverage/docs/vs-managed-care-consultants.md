# Competing with managed care consultants

How Cardinal Coverage positions against the human-services alternative — the
managed care consultant a skilled nursing facility hires to fight Medicare
Advantage denials for them. Written September 2026. Sourced where a number is
stated; no consultant fee is asserted because none is published.

## The honest picture first

A facility that is losing Medicare Advantage revenue has three moves:

| | Managed care consultant | In-house coordinator alone | Cardinal Coverage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **What you get** | Judgment, appeal expertise, plan relationships, someone to call | The person who actually files the auth and delivers the NOMNC | The system under either: every deadline computed, every plan rule dated and sourced, every outcome logged |
| **Cost shape** | Retainer or per-engagement quote; rarely published. RCM outsourcing generally runs 4–9% of net collections | ~$55K–$74K average salary, range ~$41K–$93K | Per-facility software; priced against recovered revenue |
| **When it's working** | On the days they are engaged | Every day that person is in the building | Every day, including the ones nobody is |
| **What happens when it ends** | The knowledge leaves with the engagement | The spreadsheet leaves with the coordinator | The rules registry, the outcome history and the deadline discipline stay |
| **Who it fits** | Chains and larger operators who can carry the retainer | Every facility — this role exists already | Independents who cannot afford a consultant, and consultant clients who want the work to persist between visits |

Consultants are genuinely good at three things software is not: **judgment** on
a grey clinical case, **relationships** with plan medical directors and
provider reps, and **absorbing the work** so the administrator does not have
to. Do not pretend otherwise. A pitch that disparages consultants loses the
room with any administrator who has been rescued by one.

## Where the consultant model is weak

**Hours do not compound.** A consultant who wins forty appeals leaves behind
forty won appeals. A system that tracks forty appeals leaves behind the win
rate, the plan-by-plan pattern, the exact rules each plan applied, and the
letters that worked. The second one is an asset; the first is a receipt.

**It is episodic, and the deadlines are not.** The NOMNC fast-track window
closes at noon on a specific day. The consultant is not on the floor that
morning. The coordinator is — and the coordinator needs the clock, not a
retainer.

**Priced for chains.** A retainer that makes sense across thirty buildings does
not make sense for one. North Carolina alone has 419 certified facilities, and
the independents among them are the segment consultants serve least.

**Knowledge leaves.** When the engagement ends — or the consultant's contact
moves plans, or the facility changes hands — the institutional memory of "how
Aetna actually behaves on a three-day reauth" walks out. The plan rules
registry exists precisely so it does not.

## The signal worth putting on a slide

Zimmet Healthcare — a long-established SNF consultancy — is converting its
consulting expertise into software products. When the consultants start
building the system, they are conceding that the system is the durable value.
That is not a threat to this product; it is the strongest available validation
of the category, from the people best placed to know.

## Two buyer situations, two postures

**They cannot afford a consultant.** Most independents. The pitch is direct:
this is how one building competes with a chain that has a consultant on
retainer. Your coordinator plus this system does what the chain pays a firm
for, and it never leaves.

**They already have a consultant.** Do not compete. Say: keep them, and give
them a system. Today the consultant's hours go partly to tracking — which
resident is at which day, which plan wants what. Put that in software and every
consultant hour goes to judgment, which is what you are actually paying for.
And when the engagement ends, the discipline stays.

Facilities in the second group are also the best referral source for the
first: a consultant who sees the tool working is a channel, not a competitor.

## Objection handling

**"We already have a consultant for this."**
Good — keep them. What does your consultant do between visits when a plan
issues a three-day reauth on a Friday? This is the thing that runs on Friday.
It also makes their hours cheaper, because they stop paying attention to
tracking and start paying attention to the cases that need them.

**"Our consultant already does the appeals."**
Then the appeal letters here are a template they can improve, not a
replacement. What they cannot give you is the dated record of what each plan
required at the time of the stay — that is what wins the argument three months
later when the plan claims its rule was always different.

**"Why not just hire a managed care coordinator?"**
You should. This is what that person opens in the morning. A coordinator with a
spreadsheet carries the caseload in their head; a coordinator with this carries
it in a system that survives their vacation, their resignation, and the next
plan that changes its portal.

**"A consultant knows the plans. Software doesn't."**
True, and the rules registry is how the facility captures what the consultant
knows so it stays after they leave. Ask what happens to that knowledge at the
end of the contract.

## What not to do

- Do not quote a consultant fee. None is published; a made-up number is the
  first thing a real consultant's client will catch.
- Do not claim the software replaces judgment. It replaces spreadsheets and
  missed clocks.
- Do not position against a named firm. Position against the *model* — hours
  that do not compound — and let the buyer supply the name.
