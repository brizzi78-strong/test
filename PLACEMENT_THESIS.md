# Presence as a Moat

## A supply-first, memory-care-led model for independent senior placement in a lead-mill market

*A thesis and dissertation on the design of Cardinal Promise Care Bridge*

---

## Abstract

Senior living placement in the United States is dominated by a national referral model that acquires families through paid search, converts them by telephone, distributes their contact information to multiple communities simultaneously, and collects from the community a fee of 50–100% of the resident's first month's charges. The model is commercially successful and structurally unable to do the one thing families in crisis need most: be present. This dissertation argues that an independent placement agency can build a durable local business by inverting the incumbent's design at every layer — signing supply before pursuing demand, concentrating in memory care where fees are highest and families least prepared, enforcing a minimum fee through building selection rather than contract clauses, earning referrals from channels that cannot legally be paid, and treating trust as the scarce input around which organization, geography, and compensation are arranged. The argument is developed from the specific case of a two-founder agency in Wake County, North Carolina, operating under an unusual constraint: the founder is employed in the industry and cannot front the business by day. The dissertation shows that this constraint, taken seriously, produces a better design than the unconstrained version would have. It closes with a falsifiable 90-day experiment, a 12-month scorecard, and the conditions under which the thesis should be abandoned.

---

## Thesis statement

In a market where the incumbent's advantage is scale and its weakness is absence, a local placement agency should not compete on reach. It should compete on presence, and it should organize everything — inventory, pricing, channels, partnerships, and its own founder's role — so that presence is delivered to the families who need it most at the moment they need it, by people those families and their referrers already trust. Supply must precede demand, memory care must lead, the fee floor must be enforced by selection, no referrer may ever be paid, and markets must follow trusted people rather than the reverse.

## Chapter 1 — The problem

### 1.1 The seventy-two hours

Every week, in every metropolitan hospital system, a case manager tells an adult child that their parent cannot go home. The conversation comes with a discharge date, usually two to three days out. The child has never toured an assisted living community. They do not know what memory care costs, whether their mother qualifies, or what their family can carry. They are handed a printed list of names.

This moment is the whole market. Placement is not a leisurely purchase; it is a crisis decision made under a deadline the family did not choose, by a person who is frightened, tired, and uninformed. The economic value of the placement fee — several thousand dollars per move-in — is created entirely by the difficulty of this moment. Whoever reduces the difficulty earns the fee.

### 1.2 The incumbent's answer

The dominant national referral services answer the moment with a funnel. Paid search captures the family's late-night query. A remote advisor, working by phone and commission, gathers the family's situation and transmits it to several communities in the advisor's network at once. The communities' salespeople call the family. When the family moves in, the community pays the referral service.

The design is coherent and scales beautifully. It also produces three predictable failures. The family's phone rings for days. The advisor has never walked the buildings being recommended. And the recommendation set is constrained to communities that pay, a conflict that critics and regulators have noted for years. The families experience it as being sold; the communities experience it as an expensive necessity; the discharge planners, whose referrals are the most valuable in the industry, largely refuse to hand a crying family a call center.

### 1.3 The question

If the incumbent's structural weakness is absence — of a person in the room, of local knowledge, of restraint — can an independent agency build a durable business on presence, without the incumbent's capital, and under real-world constraints on the founder's time and public profile? This dissertation argues yes, and specifies how.

## Chapter 2 — Industry context

### 2.1 Referral economics

The placement fee is paid by the community, not the family, and is customarily denominated as a percentage of the resident's first month's charges: 50–100% is the standard band, and 100% is a routinely achieved rate, not an outlier. Fees are paid after move-in, typically net 30–60, and are often subject to clawback if the resident leaves within a stated window. In the Raleigh–Durham market, assisted living averages roughly $6,350 per month and memory care roughly $7,036, before care-level fees that can add $500–2,000. A single memory care placement at 100% of total charges therefore yields $7,000–8,500.

Two features of this economics shape everything downstream. First, the fee is payable only where a signed agreement exists between the agency and the community; a placement into an unpapered building earns nothing. Second, the family pays nothing, which makes the agency's incentive structure invisible to the family unless the agency chooses to disclose it.

### 2.2 The national model in detail

The largest referral service operates a network of roughly 14,000–15,000 communities and several hundred remote advisors, and describes serving hundreds of thousands of families annually. Its unit economics match the independent agency's; its cost structure is dominated by customer acquisition through search advertising. Its advisors are compensated on volume. Its recommendations are drawn from paying inventory. It cannot economically tour with a family, and does not.

Every one of these characteristics is a rational consequence of scale. They are also the seams an independent can occupy: local presence, curated recommendations, hospital-channel credibility, and the ability to say "I have walked every building I will show you."

### 2.3 Regulatory landscape

Senior placement is lightly and unevenly regulated. A handful of states license or constrain referral agencies and require fee disclosure to families; several do not regulate the category at all. North Carolina surfaced no specific placement-agency license in the research underlying this work, a finding that should be confirmed by counsel rather than relied upon. Florida, a plausible second territory, has a Patient Brokering Act broad enough that its application to senior placement fees is a genuine question for a Florida attorney.

Beyond category-specific rules, three bodies of law constrain who an agency may pay for referrals: the federal Anti-Kickback Statute where federal healthcare program dollars touch an episode of care; state fee-splitting and medical board rules governing physicians; and professional conduct rules for attorneys, care managers, and financial advisors. Chapter 7 develops these. The summary is that the most valuable referrers in this industry cannot lawfully be paid per referral, which turns out to be a design advantage rather than a limitation.

## Chapter 3 — Theoretical frame

### 3.1 A two-sided market with supply-gated monetization

Placement is a two-sided market: communities on one side, families on the other, the agency in the middle. Unlike most two-sided platforms, monetization is gated on the supply side. Demand without signed supply is worthless; supply without demand is merely idle. This asymmetry dictates sequencing: an agency must paper the supply side before it can safely serve the demand side, or it will do its first placements for free. Most new agents chase demand first because it feels like selling. The thesis treats this as the first and most common failure mode and reverses the order.

### 3.2 Trust as the scarce input

Placement is a credence service. The family cannot evaluate the quality of the recommendation at the time of purchase; they learn whether it was right months later. In credence markets, the provider's incentive to steer is the central problem, and the standard solutions are reputation, disclosure, and costly signals of restraint.

The thesis adopts all three. Reputation is built deliberately through 30- and 90-day follow-up and retention tracking. Disclosure is made unprompted: families are told, in writing and before touring, which communities pay the agency. And restraint is signaled through a commitment device the founders can be held to — a test every recommendation must pass, named for the founder's father: *would Lou call this place home?* The device is not decoration. It is the agency's answer to the credence problem, and it costs a fee sometimes, which is what makes it credible.

### 3.3 Presence as a costly signal

The incumbent cannot tour. Touring is expensive in time, which is exactly why it is a credible signal of the agency's confidence in its recommendation. Presence — meeting the family, walking the building, being reachable within an hour — is the thesis's moat precisely because it does not scale. A moat that scaled would already belong to the incumbent.

### 3.4 Trust propagates through people, not channels

The thesis's most consequential organizational claim follows from 3.2: because the business runs on trust at every joint, the people who hold relationships are the business. This has three implications developed in Chapter 6: the founder cannot delegate the demand channel to a stranger; markets must be sequenced by where trusted people already are; and any partner who has not yet earned trust must be engaged through structures that require none.

## Chapter 4 — Supply

### 4.1 Agreements as inventory

The signed referral agreement is the agency's inventory. Twelve agreements mean turning families away; twenty-five to forty across a metro provide enough range to match any budget and care level. The target is set accordingly, and the first six weeks of the operating plan are devoted almost entirely to reaching it.

The practical mechanics favor concentration. National and regional operators sign one corporate agreement covering every building — in the Triangle, three such calls paper roughly fifteen buildings. The sequence therefore leads with chains, follows with multi-building regionals, and fills in with independents and small care homes.

### 4.2 The contract already exists

Communities that work with the national referral services hold a standard referral agency agreement. The agency does not draft; it requests, redlines, and signs. Four terms are worth negotiating: the definition of the fee base (total monthly charges including care-level fees, not base rent), the clawback window and trigger (30 days, voluntary move-out only, never death or hospitalization), payment terms (net 30), and the attribution window (6–12 months from registration). Exclusivity is never granted.

### 4.3 The fee floor: selection, not clause

The founders set a minimum of $6,500 per move-in. The thesis argues that this number belongs in the agency's building-selection rule, not in the contract. A flat fee exceeding the resident's first-month charges is unpayable — no sales director has authority to pay more than the resident pays — and the industry's paper is percentage-denominated for good reason. The policy is therefore two lines: every agreement specifies 100% of total first-month charges, and the agency signs no building whose total monthly rate falls below $6,500. The minimum is enforced by which doors the agency walks through.

The cost is honest and bounded: the agency will not be papered with the value tier. Those buildings serve the families whose money is gone, whom the agency helps at no fee regardless (Chapter 5.4). Paid inventory tilts toward mid-market assisted living and memory care — where the commitment device and the economics point together.

### 4.4 Registration: the step that makes contracts pay

A signed agreement pays only if the agency registers the family with the community before the family makes contact. Otherwise the community logs the family as an internet or walk-in lead and owes nothing. More agencies lose fees here than anywhere else. The thesis treats registration as a reflex to be trained, not a policy to be remembered.

### 4.5 Memory care concentration

Memory care leads for four reasons that reinforce one another. Every memory care move-in clears the fee floor without negotiation. Families are least prepared for it, so presence is most valuable. The founders' credentials and story already say memory care. And referrers remember specialists: "the memory care placement people" is a sharper identity than "a placement agency." Two contract terms become non-negotiable as a result — no clawback on death or hospitalization, both common in this population, and a written list of behaviors each building will and will not accept, so that no family is toured somewhere that will decline their mother at assessment.

Continuing care retirement communities are excluded from paid inventory: they fill memory care from their own residents and mostly do not pay placement fees. They are engaged instead as a demand source, since families on a CCRC waitlist whose parent needs care now are ideal clients.

## Chapter 5 — Demand

### 5.1 Channel taxonomy

Channels are ranked by the urgency and quality of the families they produce, not by volume of impressions:

| Channel | Character | Payable? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hospital case management and rehab discharge desks | Highest urgency; the 72-hour cases | Never |
| Memory clinic social workers | Every new dementia diagnosis, months earlier | Never |
| A private duty partner's in-home aides | The earliest warning system in the industry | Never (see 5.4) |
| Geriatric care managers | Private-pay by definition, pre-crisis | Never |
| Elder law attorneys | Families at the collision of money and care | Never |
| Independent living communities without their own memory care | Residents declining in place with nowhere to go | No fee involved |
| Churches, through care staff | The 2am call from a congregant | No fee involved |
| Alzheimer's Association, Dementia Alliance of NC, adult day programs | Memory care families concentrated in rooms | Sponsorship only |
| Google Business Profile, local SEO, directories, Nextdoor, reviews | The trust layer under all of the above | Free |

The striking regularity is that none of the high-value channels can be paid. This is not a constraint to route around; it is the mechanism by which the incumbent — whose only lever is money — is excluded from the channels that matter.

### 5.2 Earned reciprocity

Unpayable channels are won by two things: reliability and reciprocity. Reliability means a one-hour response, always, because a discharge planner on a 72-hour clock uses whoever answers. Reciprocity means giving each referrer something they need: the care manager gets the complex families who need oversight after move-in; the elder law attorney gets every placed family who needs a power of attorney and a benefits plan; the IL executive director gets a graceful exit for a resident she cannot keep. The thesis generalizes this as a design rule: every referrer has a problem the agency can solve for free, and the referrals follow the favor.

### 5.3 The role of the founder's story

The founder wrote a memoir about his father's death. The thesis treats this not as marketing but as the agency's credibility asset in the rooms where credibility is scarcest — churches, caregiver groups, memory cafés — and as the reason the agency's commitment device is believable. It is used with restraint and never as testimony about a family.

### 5.4 The private duty seam

The most important demand relationship in the plan is with a private duty home care company. Private duty and placement serve the same families at different moments: home care until home stops working, placement after. The home care owner has stated that he loses clients when they can no longer pay for in-home hours. Those clients divide into two tiers. Families whose money is tightening — paying $5,000–7,000 per month for aides — can usually afford assisted living at $6,350 and do not know it; they become full-fee placements, and they often save money by moving. Families whose money is gone need navigation to the state's safety net and are served at little or no fee, because "they take everyone" is the sentence that makes every other referral come.

The relationship is structured as a 90-day mutual referral pilot with no money in either direction, everything logged, and any joint venture priced on the resulting data. The structure requires no trust up front, which is why it can be offered to an acquaintance rather than a friend (Chapter 6.3).

### 5.5 What the agency does not buy

The agency does not buy search advertising against the incumbent, does not pay for listings on lead-generation directories (which are the incumbent's cousins), and does not rent influencer audiences. Each of these spends money to acquire families who are then sold to the highest bidder; the agency's entire proposition is the opposite.

## Chapter 6 — Organization

### 6.1 The founder constraint

The founder is employed in the industry and cannot front the demand channel by day or promote the business under his own name and face. A naïve reading treats this as a handicap. The thesis argues that it forced three decisions that are correct on their own merits: demand is fronted by people who are free to be public and who already stand where families stand; the brand, not a founder, is the star of every asset; and the founder's role is the one that runs from the evenings — supply, contracts, back office, content, and the pilot log. The thesis also insists on a distinction the constraint tempts one to blur: low profile is a legitimate posture, but it is not a substitute for reading the employment agreement and disclosing what it requires. Concealment discovered reads far worse than disclosure made.

### 6.2 Trust as the organizing principle

Because trust is the scarce input, the agency cannot hand its demand channel to a stranger. The founder's initial candidates for a local partner were strangers; his trusted collaborator lived in another state. The thesis resolves this with a rule — **markets follow trusted people, never the reverse** — and a sequence: the home market opens through a local partner engaged under a no-trust structure (6.3); the second territory opens where the trusted collaborator already lives, with a proven playbook rather than a cold start; further territories open only when a trusted operator exists there, through an affiliate structure written for that moment.

### 6.3 Engaging without trust

An acquaintance can be engaged through structures that make trust unnecessary until it has been demonstrated: no money changing hands, a seven-day exit, logged behavior, mutual confidentiality, narrow non-circumvention, and information discipline (the trade thesis is shared; the fee schedule and target list are not). Ninety days of logged referrals reveal more than years of friendship would, because the log shows whether the referrals convert.

### 6.4 Compensation

Anyone paid to place is paid on collected fees, never on move-in, with clawbacks passed through. Rates follow who owns the family and whose assets are used: a bird dog who hands over a name earns a flat $400–500; a part-time advisor running the family on the agency's agreements earns 40–50%; a full-time employee with salary and fed leads earns 20–30%; an out-of-market affiliate building supply and demand from zero earns 60–70%. Physicians are never paid per placement — flat advisory fees or equity only — for reasons developed in Chapter 7. Worker classification follows the facts: a genuine side worker is the cleanest 1099 case there is, and a student researcher is an hourly employee, not an unpaid intern.

### 6.5 The people who should not be hired

The thesis records two categories of help that were considered and rejected. College students on the family side — a frightened daughter does not want a twenty-year-old, and a case manager who gets one does not call back — though students on the public community-data side are defensible. And "connected people who want to make money from referrals," the most dangerous idea in the plan, because "connected" in healthcare means clinical, and clinical people cannot be paid per referral (Chapter 7).

## Chapter 7 — Legal and ethical constraints as design inputs

### 7.1 Who cannot be paid, and why it helps

The federal Anti-Kickback Statute reaches payments for referrals where federal healthcare program dollars are involved, and it reaches the payer. State fee-splitting rules and medical board ethics prohibit physicians from profiting per referral on patient care decisions regardless of payer. Bar rules prohibit attorneys from paying or accepting referral fees with non-lawyers. The Aging Life Care Association's ethics code prohibits its members from taking provider referral fees. Florida's Patient Brokering Act adds a state-level prohibition whose reach into senior placement is unresolved.

The thesis's position is that these constraints, taken together, describe the agency's competitive advantage. The channels that produce the best families are exactly the channels that money cannot buy, which means the incumbent's dominant tool is useless there and the independent's tools — reliability, reciprocity, presence — are the only ones that work.

### 7.2 Physician partners

A physician who owns a private duty company is engaged company-to-company, through the no-money pilot, and later through equity in the placement entity if the data warrant. Profit from ownership of the whole is the standard lawful structure; per-referral payment in either direction is not. The physician's own patients are never a pipeline. A physician on the advisory side receives a flat fee or equity, never a per-head payment, and the structure protects the physician's license as much as the agency.

### 7.3 Disclosure as brand

The agency's disclosure line — communities pay our fee, families never do, and we will tell you in writing which communities pay us — is simultaneously its ethical posture, its regulatory hedge in states that require fee disclosure, and its most persuasive sentence to skeptical referrers. The thesis treats this alignment as evidence that the design is sound: where compliance and marketing say the same words, the business is on solid ground.

### 7.4 Two ethical lines in marketing

Illustrative imagery is permissible; implied documentary imagery of families is not. A placeholder testimonial is not permissible at all. Both were addressed in the agency's public materials during the design period, and both are recorded here because a trust business is undone by small dishonesties before large ones.

## Chapter 8 — Method: the experiment

### 8.1 The 90-day pilot as a natural experiment

The private duty pilot is designed as an experiment with a pre-registered decision rule. Referrals are logged in both directions with source, timing, and outcome. At day 90: real flow both ways → negotiate a joint venture priced on the data; flow one way → remain at handshake referrals; no flow → part as friends at zero cost. The pilot measures the thesis's central demand claim (that in-home aides are the earliest and warmest placement lead) rather than assuming it.

### 8.2 The 90-day scorecard

| Metric | Target |
| --- | --- |
| Signed community agreements | 25–40 |
| Discharge-planner and clinic relationships | 20 |
| Second-ring professional contacts | 5–10 |
| Family inquiries | 8–15 |
| Placements | 1–3 |

Revenue is deliberately excluded as a measure in the first quarter; the cash-flow gap (8.4) makes it uninformative.

### 8.3 The 12-month scorecard

| Metric | Conservative | Base | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Placements per month, exit rate | 3–4 | 5–6 | 8+ |
| Cumulative placements | 30 | 45 | 65 |
| Average fee (under the $6,500 floor) | $6,500 | $7,000 | $7,500 |
| Year-one revenue | ~$195K | ~$315K | ~$490K |

### 8.4 The cash-flow gap

The agency will work two to four months before its first check clears: inquiry, tours, move-in, invoice, net-30-to-60 payment. This single fact kills more placement agencies than a shortage of leads. The founder's employment income during the build is therefore not merely a constraint but the plan's runway, which is one more reason the constraint improved the design.

### 8.5 The frustration log

From day one the founders record every repetitive manual task. After twelve months this log is the only legitimate basis for a decision to build software — a path the original strategy gated on interviewing twenty independent agents and obtaining paid commitments before writing code. The thesis retains that gate.

## Chapter 9 — Sensitivity

The projections are most sensitive to one variable: the number of signed buildings that clear the fee floor. Under a strict 100%-of-total-charges policy, some national chains will decline at the local level because rates are fixed at corporate. The plan treats each "no" as "not yet," logs it, and returns at month four with move-ins behind it. If the signed count at day 90 falls below twenty, the thesis predicts inventory-constrained placements and recommends widening the geographic ring before loosening the floor.

The second sensitivity is the partner liaison's actual route. If the private duty company's community liaison does not, in practice, visit rehab and hospital discharge desks weekly, the plan's daytime demand channel is thinner than assumed and the founder's transition out of employment becomes the binding constraint on growth.

The third is memory care acceptance. Behavioral exclusions at assessment can turn a registered referral into a non-placement; the written acceptance list in each agreement (4.5) is the mitigation, and the rate of declined assessments should be tracked from the first placement.

## Chapter 10 — Limitations and risks

**Single-market evidence.** The design is derived from one metro's economics; fee and rent figures are research-based planning numbers, not quotes, and should be replaced with the state licensing roster and first-call data.

**Regulatory uncertainty.** The absence of a North Carolina placement-agency license was not confirmed by counsel. The Florida question is open. Both belong on a single attorney engagement that has now accumulated seven agenda items.

**Key-person and partner dependency.** The demand channel initially runs through a partner's employee. Non-solicitation, company-name agreements, a company phone number, and a company CRM limit but do not eliminate the risk.

**Reputational contagion.** The founders' other ventures include a cryptocurrency token whose launch materials were merged into the shared repository during the design period. Discharge planners, frightened families, and physician partners all read "crypto" as a negative signal on a senior care brand. The thesis recommends, without qualification, that any such venture live under a separate name and domain with no visible link to the agency.

**The founder's transition.** The plan assumes the founder eventually fronts the home market personally. The date is unknown, and the plan's second and third phases are calibrated to a person who does not yet exist in the daytime.

## Chapter 11 — Conclusion

The incumbent built a national business on the premise that placement is a lead to be captured and resold. This dissertation argues that placement is a moment to be present for, and that a local agency organized entirely around presence can occupy the seams scale leaves open. The design's elements are not independent: supply-first sequencing makes the fee floor enforceable; the fee floor makes memory care the natural focus; memory care makes the credential and the story relevant; the story earns the unpayable channels; the unpayable channels exclude the incumbent; and the founder's constraint, which forced the business to run through trusted people rather than through its founder's face, is what makes the whole structure survivable if any one person steps away.

The thesis is falsifiable. If, at day 90, fewer than twenty buildings have signed, the private duty log shows no flow, and no discharge planner has called back, the model has failed on its own terms and should be abandoned rather than adjusted. If those three numbers land, the rest of the year is execution.

*A place Lou would call home.*
