# Verifiable Restraint

### Costly Commitment as the Operating Discipline of Well-Run Digital-Asset Firms, 2020–2026

A dissertation in the economics of organizations and financial technology

August 2026

---

## Abstract

**Problem.** The digital-asset sector destroys firms at a rate without parallel in modern technology
industries, and it does so in a distinctive pattern: failures are frequently total and sudden rather
than gradual. Roughly 176 projects closed in 2025 and a further ~100 in the first half of 2026, and
several of the largest failures of the preceding cycle were firms that observers had rated highly
shortly before collapse. Existing commentary on which crypto firms are "well run" is almost entirely
outcome-based, and therefore has no predictive content.

**Question.** This dissertation asks whether there exist observable, ex-ante properties that
distinguish firms which survive and retain market access from those which do not, and if so, what
economic mechanism explains their effect.

**Theory.** The dissertation argues that the sector is a severe adverse-selection market in the
sense of Akerlof (1970): counterparties cannot distinguish solvent, honestly-run firms from
fraudulent ones, and the informational asymmetry is compounded by the irreversibility of settlement.
Under these conditions, the standard resolution is costly signaling (Spence, 1973; Zahavi, 1975).
The dissertation's central construct, **verifiable restraint**, is a specific class of costly signal
in which a firm irreversibly narrows its own future action set — through segregated custody,
recurring third-party attestation, licensure ahead of requirement, or machine-enforced on-chain
constraints — thereby raising the cost of its own future misbehavior in a manner that
counterparties can verify without trusting the firm. This is the commitment device of Schelling
(1960) implemented in code and law rather than in reputation.

**Method.** A theory-building multiple-case study (Eisenhardt, 1989; Yin, 2018) of twenty-four firms
observed 2020–2026, combining public filings, regulatory actions, on-chain data, and trade
reporting. Failure cases are analysed for the *absence* of specified controls rather than for the
presence of shared features, a design chosen to mitigate the undersampling-of-failure bias
identified by Denrell (2003).

**Findings.** Eight propositions are derived, each stated with the observation that would falsify
it. The failure population is shown to cluster on three mechanisms — revenue endogenous to the firm's
own token, commingled customer assets, and reserves sized to the modal rather than the maximal loss
— which together account for the substantial majority of observed terminal events. A ten-item
instrument operationalising the framework is presented and applied to six firms and one small
launch.

**Contribution and limits.** The dissertation's contribution is a falsifiable, ex-ante construct
where the existing literature offers ex-post description. Its principal limitation is that the
strongest observed counter-case — a firm earning approximately $10bn annually while failing the
central condition — is not explained away but is used to narrow the thesis: verifiable restraint is
shown to govern *market access and tail survival*, not profitability. Chapter 13 details the
remaining threats to validity, of which small-N inference and a favourable 2025–26 US regulatory
shift are the most serious.

## Thesis statement

> In digital-asset markets, where settlement is irreversible and counterparty quality is
> unobservable, firms cannot credibly communicate solvency and honest intent through assertion.
> The firms that survive and retain market access are those that instead purchase credibility
> through **verifiable restraint** — costly, irreversible, third-party-checkable constraints on
> their own future conduct. Such restraint is not a tax on operating speed but the principal
> mechanism by which durable firms are separated from indistinguishable ones, and its adoption
> predicts survival and market access even though it does not predict profitability.

## A note on evidence

Empirical claims are cited at the point of use. Where a claim is load-bearing, a primary source is
used: SEC filings, company press releases, statutory text, or government notices. Private-company
valuations and revenues rest on trade reporting, frequently disagree between outlets by margins
approaching a factor of two, and are reported as ranges and treated as directional throughout.

Scholarly citations follow author-date form in text with full references in
[Chapter 15](15-bibliography.md). Bibliographic details for classical works are given in standard
form and should be verified against the originals before any formal submission.
