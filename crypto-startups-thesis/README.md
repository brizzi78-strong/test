# What Separates the Best-Run Crypto Startups

A thesis and dissertation on operating discipline in digital-asset companies, 2020–2026.

---

## Thesis

**The best-run crypto startups are the ones that treat crypto as an input to a business rather than as the business itself.**

They earn revenue from customers who would keep paying if the token price went to zero. They hold
themselves to disclosure and control standards ahead of what their regulator currently requires.
And they are engineered so that a single catastrophic technical failure is survivable rather than
terminal.

The corollary is the part most founders resist: in this industry, governance discipline is not a
tax on crypto-native speed. It is the thing that compounds. Trust is the scarcest input in a market
whose entire premise is that you should not have to trust anyone, and verifiable restraint —
renounced keys, locked liquidity, published reserves, segregated customer assets, licenses obtained
before they were required — is the cheapest way to buy it. Every firm in this study that is still
standing bought it early. Most of the firms that are gone tried to buy it later, at the moment they
needed it, when the price had become infinite.

## Findings in brief

1. **Revenue durability beats revenue size.** Coinbase's subscription and services line went from
   29% of net revenue to 48% in under two years and is what let it post growth through the 2026
   drawdown ([Q2 2026 results](https://www.morningstar.com/news/business-wire/20260730470121/coinbase-q2-earnings-everything-exchange-drives-3rd-consecutive-quarter-of-record-crypto-trading-volume-market-share-revenue-diversification-and-resilience)).
   The firms that died in 2025–26 were, with few exceptions, firms whose revenue was a derivative of
   their own token price.
2. **Custody separation is the single highest-value control.** It is the one thing FTX, Celsius, and
   BlockFi all lacked, and the one thing Bybit had when it lost $1.5 billion in a night and processed
   every withdrawal anyway.
3. **Distribution economics are usually the real business model, and the well-run firms disclose
   them.** Circle pays out roughly 59% of gross revenue in distribution and transaction costs
   ([Q2 2026](https://www.circle.com/pressroom/circle-reports-second-quarter-2026-results)). That
   disclosed weakness is worth more than an undisclosed strength.
4. **Regulatory posture is a moat that only pays late.** Every dollar Circle, Coinbase, Anchorage,
   and Kraken spent on licensure between 2018 and 2024 looked like waste until the
   [GENIUS Act](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/1582) made it table stakes
   in July 2025.
5. **Headcount discipline is real but is not a virtue by itself.** Tether earns roughly $100M of
   profit per employee. It also cannot list on a US exchange. Efficiency and governance are separate
   axes, and the market prices them separately.
6. **The highest risk-adjusted outcome in the sector is now an infrastructure acquisition, not a
   token.** Bridge raised $58M and sold to Stripe for $1.1B in under three years without ever
   issuing a token.
7. **Crisis response is a governance artifact, not a PR exercise.** You cannot decide during the
   incident whether you kept enough excess reserves to cover it.

## Scope and honesty about it

This is a qualitative study of roughly two dozen firms, built from public filings, press reporting,
and on-chain data available through August 2026. It is not a statistical result. The sample is
biased toward US and EU firms with public disclosure, which is exactly the population most likely to
confirm a thesis about disclosure being good. Chapter 01 says more about what that does to the
conclusions, and Chapter 05 makes the strongest case I can against the thesis rather than the
weakest.

Figures are cited to their sources at the point of use. Where reporting conflicts — and for
valuations and private-company revenue it frequently does — the range is given rather than a point
estimate.
