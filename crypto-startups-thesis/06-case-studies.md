# 6. Case studies

*Eight cases drawn from the sampling frames of §4.3, each selected because it isolates a different
variable. §6.1 and §6.8 bear on H4, §6.7 on H3, §6.3 and §6.5 on H1. Where a case cuts against the
thesis, that is said rather than smoothed over; the case that cuts hardest is held for Chapter 8.*

Sections are numbered in order below. Each closes with a reading against the four conditions of §3.3.

## 6.1 Circle — the disclosure trade, priced in public

**What it isolates:** whether disclosure-ahead-of-requirement actually pays, and what it costs.

Circle spent years doing the expensive, unglamorous version of stablecoin issuance: US money
transmitter licences, monthly reserve attestations, reserves in short-dated Treasuries and cash at
regulated institutions, and a public campaign for the regulation that would eventually bind it. For
most of that period the strategy looked strictly worse than the alternative. Tether was larger, far
more profitable, and carried none of the cost.

The verdict arrived in public. Circle listed on the NYSE on 5 June 2025 at $31 per share, above the
$27–28 indicated range, and closed the first day at $83.23 — a 168% gain, raising over $1.1 billion
and valuing the company above $16 billion
([BingX summary](https://bingx.com/en/learn/article/circle-ipo-about-crcl-valuation-and-what-it-means-for-the-crypto-market),
[Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/06/06/circle-internet-group-ipo-crcl-underpricing-stock-outlook-profits/)).
It peaked at a $263.45 close on 23 June 2025 and has round-tripped since, closing at $62.61 on 31
July 2026 ([Macrotrends](https://www.macrotrends.net/stocks/charts/CRCL/circle-internet/stock-price-history),
[stockanalysis](https://stockanalysis.com/stocks/crcl/)). Anyone who bought the story at the top has
lost money. The company is not the stock.

The operating business through 2026:

| | Q1 2026 | Q2 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue + reserve income | $694M (+20% YoY) | $701M (+7% YoY) |
| Distribution, transaction & other costs | $407M (+17%) | $412M (+1%) |
| Adjusted EBITDA | $151M (+24%), 53% margin | — (swung to net profit; guidance raised) |

Sources: [Circle Q1 2026](https://www.circle.com/pressroom/circle-reports-first-quarter-2026-results),
[Circle Q2 2026](https://www.circle.com/pressroom/circle-reports-second-quarter-2026-results).

The interesting line is distribution cost. Circle pays out roughly 59% of gross revenue to
distribution partners — overwhelmingly to Coinbase, which is compensated for USDC held on its
platform. That is not a footnote; it is the business model. Circle does not primarily sell a
stablecoin to users. It rents shelf space from whoever controls the users, and keeps the spread on
the float.

Two observations follow. First, this is a structurally weak position, and Circle discloses it every
quarter in a table anyone can read. That disclosure is the thesis working as designed: a legible
weakness that investors can price beats an illegible strength. Second, it explains Circle's 2026
behavior — the ARC chain and its $222 million token presale
([8-K](https://www.stocktitan.net/sec-filings/CRCL/8-k-circle-internet-group-inc-reports-material-event-a080242d188f.html))
is an attempt to own distribution rather than rent it. Whether that works is the open question in
the name.

There is also a macro exposure worth naming plainly: reserve income is interest income, so Circle's
revenue is levered to policy rates. A rate-cutting cycle compresses it regardless of how well the
company is run. Good operators do not get to opt out of their input prices.

**Reads on the four conditions:** revenue independence — strong on customer identity, weak on rate
exposure and distribution concentration. Segregation — strong, and the reason it could list.
Survivability — strong. Disclosure — best in class, and it was the moat.

## 6.2 Coinbase — diversification as an operating discipline

**What it isolates:** whether a firm whose core business is inherently cyclical can engineer its way
out of the cycle.

Coinbase's problem for its entire public life has been that trading revenue is a function of
volatility and price, neither of which it controls. Its answer has been to build revenue lines that
are not. FY2025 net revenue was $6.9 billion
([FY2025 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001679788/000167978826000047/coinbase2025ars.pdf)).
The structural change shows up in the mix:

- Subscription and services was 44% of net revenue in Q1 2026 and 48% in Q2 2026, up from 29% less
  than two years earlier.
- That line grew from $6 million in Q2 2020 to $555 million in Q2 2026.
- Twelve separate products now generate over $100 million in annualized revenue.
- Bitcoin trading fell to roughly 12% of revenue.

Sources: [Coinbase Q2 2026 release](https://www.morningstar.com/news/business-wire/20260730470121/coinbase-q2-earnings-everything-exchange-drives-3rd-consecutive-quarter-of-record-crypto-trading-volume-market-share-revenue-diversification-and-resilience),
[Coin Metrics analysis of Q1 2026](https://coinmetrics.substack.com/p/coinbase-q1-2026-earnings-diversification),
[BigGo summary of the Q2 call](https://finance.biggo.com/news/US_COIN_2026-07-30).

The Deribit acquisition is the clearest single expression of the strategy: $2.9 billion in May 2025,
structured as $700 million cash plus 11 million Class A shares, closed August 2025, bringing $59
billion of open interest, over $1 trillion in annual volume, and roughly $30 million of transaction
revenue in July alone ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/366957/coinbase-completes-2-9-billion-cash-and-stock-acquisition-of-deribit),
[Coinbase](https://www.coinbase.com/blog/deribit-joins-coinbase-unlocking-the-future-of-global-crypto-derivatives)).
Paying more than half the consideration in stock at a moment when your stock is strong, for an asset
whose revenue is counter-cyclical to your own, is textbook capital allocation.

The honest counterweight: Q2 2026 still missed revenue estimates by about $70 million, and the
subscription segment missed too
([Techtimes](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/322358/20260730/coinbase-q2-revenue-miss-widens-70m-subscription-segment-also-falls-short.htm)).
Diversification bought a buffer, not immunity. And "subscription and services" is a heterogeneous
bucket — it includes stablecoin distribution revenue from Circle, which is itself a rate bet, and
blockchain rewards, which are an asset-price bet. The mix is genuinely more durable than it was. It
is less durable than the label implies.

**Reads:** revenue independence — much improved and openly measured. Segregation — strong.
Survivability — strong. Disclosure — strong, including the misses.

## 6.3 Bridge — the highest risk-adjusted outcome in the sector

**What it isolates:** what the best exit actually looks like now, and how little capital it took.

Bridge was founded in 2022 by Zach Abrams and Sean Yu, who had previously built Evenly and sold it
to Square in 2013. It built one thing: an API letting a business accept, hold, convert, and pay out
stablecoins through a single integration. No token. No airdrop. No community. Customers included
SpaceX, Coinbase, and the US Treasury Department, and it crossed $5 billion in annualized payment
volume.

It raised $58 million total and was valued at $200 million in a $40 million Series A in 2024. Stripe
announced its acquisition on 21 October 2024 for $1.1 billion and closed on 4 February 2025
([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/05/stripe-makes-1-1-billion-crypto-bet-as-it-closes-on-bridge-acquisition/),
[CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/02/04/stripe-closes-1point1-billion-bridge-deal-prepares-for-stablecoin-push-.html)).
Roughly 5.5x the last private mark, on $58 million of lifetime capital, inside three years.

Stripe followed it in June 2025 by acquiring Privy, founded 2021, which had raised about $40 million
and supported 75 million wallets across 1,000 applications
([CoinDesk](https://www.coindesk.com/business/2025/06/11/stripe-to-acquire-crypto-wallet-startup-privy-in-bid-to-expand-web3-capabilities)).
Same shape: developer infrastructure, boring surface, no token, acquired by a payments incumbent
that needed the capability and could not build it fast enough.

The lesson is uncomfortable for the token-native worldview. Both companies captured a large fraction
of the value they created, in cash, with a fraction of the capital and none of the regulatory
exposure that a token would have attached. The pattern's ceiling is lower than a Circle or a
Coinbase. Its expected value, adjusted for the base rate in Chapter 5, is much higher.

**Reads:** revenue independence — total; customers paid for a service. Segregation — n/a to the
model. Survivability — n/a. Disclosure — private, but the acquirer's diligence substituted.

## 6.4 Tether — profit without any of the rest

**What it isolates:** the strongest evidence against the thesis. Treated at length in
[Chapter 8](08-counter-case.md); the facts are recorded here.

Tether reported over $10 billion in net profit for 2025, against $186.5 billion of USDT liabilities,
with $6.3 billion in excess reserves, up to $141 billion of US Treasury exposure, more than 140 tons
of gold worth about $17.4 billion, and roughly $8.4 billion of bitcoin. The attestation was
published on 30 January 2026 and performed by BDO
([Tether](https://tether.io/news/tether-delivers-10b-profits-in-2025-6-3b-in-excess-reserves-and-record-141-billion-exposure-in-u-s-treasury-holdings/),
[CoinDesk](https://www.coindesk.com/business/2026/01/30/tether-s-gold-holdings-top-usd17-billion-as-net-profits-surpassed-usd10-billion-for-2025)).

It does this with roughly 100 employees. That is about $100 million of profit per employee — a ratio
with no real precedent in any industry.

An attestation is not an audit. Gold and bitcoin in the reserve of a dollar-pegged instrument are a
duration and volatility mismatch that a US-regulated issuer could not run. And the company's
domicile history is a governance choice, not an accident. All of that is true simultaneously with
the profit figure, which is the point of the case.

## 6.5 Fireblocks and Chainalysis — selling the shovels, and who pays for them

**What it isolates:** whether the "infrastructure is safer" claim survives a bear market.

Fireblocks provides MPC-based custody and transfer infrastructure to banks, exchanges, and funds. It
has raised about $1.04 billion at a valuation near $8 billion and secures roughly $8.1 billion in
staked assets. Chainalysis sells blockchain analytics to banks, exchanges, and government agencies.
Both sell to institutions whose need is regulatory rather than speculative, which is the specific
property that makes their revenue survive drawdowns: a bank's obligation to screen transactions does
not decrease when bitcoin falls.

This is condition 1 in its purest form. The customer's willingness to pay is causally independent of
the asset price, because it is driven by a legal obligation.

The qualification worth stating: infrastructure revenue is independent of price but not independent
of *industry headcount*. When exchanges consolidate and 100 projects shut down in a half-year, the
seat count falls. Infrastructure is a lagging casualty of a bear market, not an exempt one. The
firms in this category that have done best have pushed toward regulated financial institutions,
whose seat counts are stable, and away from crypto-native customers, whose seat counts are not.

## 6.6 Kraken — discipline includes not going public

**What it isolates:** whether a well-run firm can be recognized by what it declines to do.

Kraken doubled revenue to $1.5 billion in 2024, filed a confidential draft S-1 in November 2025, and
raised $800 million at a $20 billion valuation in a round led by Citadel Securities, targeting a Q1
2026 listing. In March 2026 it paused the listing, citing market conditions
([Forge](https://forgeglobal.com/insights/kraken-upcoming-ipo-news/),
[FinancialContent](https://markets.financialcontent.com/wral/article/marketminute-2025-12-25-kraken-eyes-2026-public-debut-surpassing-15-billion-valuation-as-crypto-ipo-fever-returns)).

Pausing was the right call and it is instructive precisely because it looks like a setback. The 2025
IPO window was real — crypto firms raised $3.4 billion through listings that year, with Bullish
debuting on the NYSE in August 2025 and Gemini on Nasdaq in September
([DL News](https://www.dlnews.com/articles/markets/six-blockbuster-crypto-ipos-to-watch-in-2026/)) —
and by early 2026 it was not. A firm with $800 million of fresh capital and no obligation to list
had the option to wait. Firms that need the listing do not have that option, which is why they list
into bad windows and why the ones that do are usually the ones you should worry about.

The general form: **optionality is an operating asset, and it is created by not needing money.**

## 6.7 Bybit — the crisis-response case

**What it isolates:** whether pre-committed reserves convert a catastrophe into an incident.

On 21 February 2025, attackers took approximately $1.5 billion in ETH from Bybit, the largest crypto
theft on record. The mechanism was a compromised third-party service used to move tokens from cold
to warm storage, with malicious JavaScript injected into the signing flow — meaning the signers
approved what looked like a legitimate transaction. The FBI attributed it to North Korea five days
later ([FBI](https://www.fbi.gov/investigate/cyber/alerts/2025/north-korea-responsible-for-1-5-billion-bybit-hack),
[TRM Labs](https://www.trmlabs.com/resources/blog/the-bybit-hack-following-north-koreas-largest-exploit)).

The response is the case. Bybit stated within hours that reserves still exceeded liabilities and
that user funds remained fully backed. It processed all withdrawals and resumed normal operations in
under twelve hours, with roughly $4 billion flowing into the platform in that window, covering the
shortfall. It published a running incident timeline and launched a recovery bounty of up to 10% of
recovered funds ([Bybit timeline](https://learn.bybit.com/en/this-week-in-bybit/bybit-security-incident-timeline),
[Chainalysis](https://www.chainalysis.com/blog/bybit-exchange-hack-february-2025-crypto-security-dprk/)).

Two lessons, in order of importance.

First, and this is the whole point: **the outcome was determined before the attack.** Bybit could
say "we are still solvent" because it had capital in excess of the largest theft in history sitting
idle for exactly this purpose. That capital had a cost, paid every quarter for years, in a business
where competitors deployed theirs. Chapter 5's failure mode #3 is the same event at a firm that had
not paid it.

Second, the failure itself is a governance finding, not just a technical one. The compromise came
through a third party in the signing path. Every hardware and process control Bybit had was
defeated by trusting one vendor's front-end. That is a supply-chain control gap, and it is the
single most transferable technical lesson in this document: *your key-management security is the
security of the least-examined component in the signing path, including the ones you did not write.*

## 6.8 Kalshi and Polymarket — does the pattern generalize to a new category?

**What it isolates:** whether these findings are about crypto or about 2021–2024 specifically.

Prediction markets scaled to roughly $21 billion in monthly volume during 2026
([TRM Labs](https://www.trmlabs.com/resources/blog/how-prediction-markets-scaled-to-usd-21b-in-monthly-volume-in-2026)),
with estimated fee revenue between $850 million and $1.5 billion annualized. Kalshi raised over $1
billion in March 2026 at a $22 billion valuation, having been at $11 billion in December 2025, and
was reportedly back out at $40 billion by Q3. Polymarket took a strategic investment of up to $2
billion from ICE at an $8 billion valuation in October 2025, completed in March 2026, and has been
discussed in the $9–15 billion range
([Value Add VC](https://valueaddvc.com/blog/kalshi-vs-polymarket-valuation-2026-22b-vs-15b-and-which-prediction-market-is-winning),
[GI](https://company.gi/blog/prediction-markets-institutional-land-grab-2026)).

The structural point: Kalshi built inside CFTC regulation from the start, litigated for the right to
list its contracts, and by June 2026 was doing roughly $31.5 billion in monthly volume against
Polymarket's $10.8 billion. Polymarket, which had operated offshore, obtained a CFTC no-action
letter in January 2026 to re-enter the US.

Kalshi's regulatory-first posture is exactly the Circle strategy applied to a different asset class,
and it produced the same result: a period of looking slower, followed by an unassailable position at
the moment the category became legitimate. That the pattern reproduces in a category that barely
existed when the earlier cases were decided is the best available evidence that these findings are
about structure rather than about one cycle.

The caution: both valuations embed enormous growth expectations, and the category's revenue is
volume-linked, which means it is sentiment-linked, which means condition 1 is weaker here than the
headline numbers suggest. Ask again in 2028.
