# 13. Limitations and threats to validity

Stated in descending order of severity. Each names what would be required to address it.

## 13.1 Small-N inference and the impossibility of separating luck from skill

The study observes twenty-four firms in a sector whose returns are extremely fat-tailed. In such a
distribution, a small sample cannot distinguish managerial quality from favourable draw, and no
qualitative design can repair this.

The concrete form of the problem: Circle's listing preceded the GENIUS Act by roughly six weeks;
Coinbase funded the Deribit acquisition largely in stock at a favourable price; Kalshi's regulatory
position benefited from a CFTC chair withdrawing restrictive proposed rules in January 2026. Each is
consistent with the framework and each is also consistent with timing.

**Partial defence.** The failure analysis is more robust than the success analysis, because the
failure population clusters on a small number of identifiable absent controls while the successes
are heterogeneous (§4.2). The dissertation is therefore more securely a statement about **what
reliably terminates these firms** than about what reliably makes them excellent, and should be read
with that asymmetry in mind.

**What would address it.** A larger panel with survival analysis over the full population of firms
raising above a capital threshold in a defined window, coding C1–C4 at raise date and measuring time
to terminal event. This is feasible and is proposed in §14.4.

## 13.2 Selection on the dependent variable of the central hypothesis

Frame 1 (§4.3) over-samples firms that completed public listings, which are by construction firms
that disclose. The thesis holds that disclosure predicts market access. Sampling firms selected for
disclosing and observing that they obtained access is close to circular.

**Partial defence.** Three mitigations. Frames 2 and 3 include private firms facing no disclosure
requirement, and the framework does not perform uniformly across them — Bridge scores 67 while
producing the study's best financial outcome, which is a disconfirming observation reported rather
than suppressed (§9.4). Frame 4 supplies out-of-sample replication in a category that had not
resolved when the theory was formed. And Chapter 8 selects the case most damaging to the thesis and
narrows the claim rather than defending it.

**What would address it.** Sampling on the independent variable: identify firms by their disclosure
posture at time *t* and measure access outcomes at *t+n*, including firms that disclosed and failed.

## 13.3 The regulatory window is a confound

The observation period contains an unusually favourable shift in US policy: the GENIUS Act enacted
July 2025, the CLARITY Act pending, a CFTC posture reversal on prediction markets, and a listing
window that opened in 2025 and closed by early 2026. Firms that had adopted restraint early were
rewarded partly by that shift, and the mechanism proposed here would predict the same rewards for
different reasons. The two are not separable within this window.

**Partial defence.** Each proposition in Chapter 7 carries an explicit exposure rating; P3 and P8
are flagged as moderate and high respectively. P2, P5 and P6 concern mechanisms — run dynamics,
absorbing barriers, integration security — that are regime-independent.

**What would address it.** Replication in a jurisdiction whose regulatory trajectory diverged over
the same period, and re-testing after the CLARITY Act resolves.

## 13.4 Single-coder design

All coding was performed by one analyst with knowledge of the outcomes. There is no inter-rater
reliability statistic, and the risk that item scores were assigned partly from known outcomes rather
than from the specified anchors cannot be excluded.

**Partial defence.** Appendix A publishes every item score and the coding rules, so a reader may
recode any firm and locate disagreement on a specific line rather than in aggregate. This makes the
bias auditable, not absent.

**What would address it.** Independent coding by analysts blinded to firm identity and outcome, with
a reported agreement statistic.

## 13.5 Evidence quality for private firms

Roughly one third of citations are trade press. Private-company valuations and revenues frequently
differ between outlets by margins approaching a factor of two, and are used here only where nothing
better exists. The coding convention that treats absent evidence as absent capability (§4.5)
systematically penalises private firms; §9.4 estimates the effect at 10–20 points but this estimate
is itself a judgment rather than a measurement.

## 13.6 Construct validity of the instrument

The ten-item scorecard has not been validated. Its weights were assigned by the author from the
framework rather than derived from data, no factor structure has been tested, and no predictive
validity has been established against subsequent outcomes. It is a formalisation of the argument,
not a measurement instrument, and should not be described as one.

The arithmetic is at least now internally consistent: weights sum to 50, items score 0–2, and the
maximum is 100. An earlier draft of this instrument asserted a 100-point maximum over weights summing
to 72 — an error of scale that inflated the reported score of the application case in Chapter 10 by
four points before correction. The episode is recorded here because it illustrates §13.4: a
single-coder design has no mechanism to catch such an error except recomputation.

## 13.7 Endogeneity between conditions

C1 and C3 are not independent. Reserves sized to maximal loss are affordable only to firms with
revenue independent of the asset price, as §12.3 notes. The framework treats the four conditions as
separable for expositional clarity, but at least one dependency is structural, which means the
instrument double-counts to an unknown degree.

## 13.8 Scope exclusions

Protocols without a controlling firm, DAOs, and pure trading entities are excluded (§1.5). If a
meaningful share of durable value in the sector accrues to these forms, the framework addresses a
shrinking subset of the phenomenon. The Aramonte, Huang and Schrimpf (2021) decentralisation-illusion
argument is the basis for thinking otherwise, but it is an assumption the study inherits rather than
tests.
