# 14. Conclusion

## 14.1 Answers to the research questions

**RQ1 — Do observable ex-ante properties predict survival and market access?** Yes, and they are
few. The failure population clusters on three mechanisms: revenue endogenous to the firm's own
token, customer assets reachable by the firm's own losses, and reserves sized to the modal rather
than the maximal loss (Chapter 5). Each is observable before the outcome. The corresponding positive
conditions — C1, C2, C3 — together with disclosure ahead of requirement (C4) constitute the
framework's answer.

**RQ2 — What mechanism accounts for their effect?** Costly signaling under adverse selection. In a
market where settlement is irreversible and counterparty quality is private information, assertions
are pooling and convey nothing. Verifiable restraint — irreversible, third-party-checkable
narrowing of the firm's own action set — separates types because it is differentially more expensive
for firms that intend to misbehave (§3.1). Public-blockchain settlement supplies a mechanically
enforced form of this commitment that is stronger than anything available to conventional firms and,
unusually, is available cheaply at any scale.

**RQ3 — Can this be operationalised?** Yes, with heavy caveats. Chapter 9's instrument formalises
the framework into ten weighted items applied to six firms. Its construct validity is unestablished
(§13.6) and it is best understood as a structured argument rather than a measurement.

**RQ4 — Does the framework hold at small scale?** Partially, and the failure is instructive. A firm
without a balance sheet cannot purchase C3 at all, capping its attainable score near 94 (§10.6). But
the mechanically enforced constraints of C4 remain fully available and nearly free, which produces
the sector's characteristic pathology: small projects score maximum marks on the cheapest signal and
zero on the expensive one, and the market has learned to read the cheap signal as sufficient.
Chapter 10 shows a well-constructed launch scoring 48/100 on exactly this pattern.

## 14.2 The thesis, in its final and narrowed form

The initial claim was that governance discipline compounds. Chapter 8 shows this is too strong. A
firm earning approximately $10bn annually while failing C4 is not an anomaly to be explained away;
it establishes that the mechanism does not operate on profitability at all.

The claim that survives:

> Verifiable restraint is not what makes a digital-asset firm profitable. It determines which
> markets remain open to it, how much of its profit it retains across a full cycle, and whether a
> single catastrophic night is survivable. Where the left tail is absorbing, that is a defensible
> objective function — but it is a *different* objective function from expected return, and a
> founder should choose it deliberately rather than assume the two coincide.

The counter-case also identifies the boundary condition: network effects in a monetary instrument
can dominate governance for a very long time. A firm that achieves such a position may sustain a low
score indefinitely. Almost no firm achieves one, which is why the ungoverned path is better
described as a story about someone who did than as an available strategy.

## 14.3 Practical implications

For founders, the ordering follows from §13.7's dependency rather than from preference. C1 must come
first because it funds everything else: one customer, paying in ordinary currency, for an outcome
they need irrespective of the asset price. Optionality follows from revenue; the reserve follows
from optionality. A project cannot begin at the reserve.

Two constraints should be adopted immediately regardless of scale, because both are close to free
and one closes a failure mode that is irreversible and total: the mechanical constraints of P8, and
the signing-path discipline of P6. Chapter 10 quantifies the latter as the highest-return item
available to a small operator.

For counterparties and allocators, the instrument's most useful property is its worst-performing
item. A zero on P2 is effectively disqualifying regardless of the aggregate, because it is the
failure mode with no gradual form.

## 14.4 Further research

**A survival panel.** The strongest available response to §13.1. Code C1–C4 at raise date for the
full population of firms raising above a capital threshold in a defined window; measure time to
terminal event. This converts the study's central claims into testable hazard ratios and is feasible
with existing data.

**Instrument validation.** Blinded multi-coder application, factor analysis of the ten items, and
predictive validity testing against subsequent outcomes. §13.7 suggests the items are not
independent and that a shorter instrument may carry the same information.

**Post-CLARITY replication.** P8 carries high regulatory exposure. Re-testing the token propositions
after the market-structure legislation resolves is the natural extension, and would also supply the
regime variation §13.3 requires.

**The private-firm disclosure puzzle.** Bridge and Privy achieved the study's best outcomes while
scoring poorly on C4, for structural reasons. Whether voluntary disclosure has any return for firms
whose exit is acquisition rather than listing is an open question this study cannot answer and its
sample cannot address.

## 14.5 Closing

The sector's founding premise is that trust should be unnecessary. The finding of this dissertation
is close to the inverse: because trust is unnecessary in the protocol, it becomes scarce and
expensive at the level of the firm, and the firms that endure are those that purchase it by
surrendering the capacity to betray it.

Eight of the ten items in the final instrument would read unremarkably in a study of well-run
custody banks. That is not a deflationary observation. It is the thesis arriving at its destination:
the best-run digital-asset firms treat the technology as an input to a business, and the closer a
firm approaches the maximal score, the more it resembles a conservative financial institution that
happens to settle on public infrastructure.
