# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 The problem

In November 2022 a reasonable observer compiling a list of the best-run firms in the digital-asset
sector would have placed FTX near the top of it. The firm had revenue, growth, brand-name venture
investors, a stated regulatory strategy, and a founder who had testified before the United States
Congress on consumer protection. Within weeks it was insolvent, and the insolvency was not the
result of a market movement but of an accounting structure that had been in place throughout the
period in which the firm was admired.

This is the problem the dissertation addresses. Assessments of managerial quality in this sector are
made almost entirely from outcomes — size, growth, funding, prominence — and outcomes in this sector
are a poor guide to process, because the failure mode that matters most is invisible until it is
total. A framework with any practical value must be constructible from information available
*before* the outcome is known.

The stakes are not merely analytical. Between January 2025 and July 2026 approximately 275 projects
ceased operations, including centralised exchanges that had survived the 2022 collapse intact
(Chapter 5). Venture funding into the sector contracted by roughly half quarter-on-quarter in Q1
2026. Founders, employees, and counterparties are making allocation decisions in a market where the
base rate of total loss is high and the observable signals are weak.

## 1.2 Research questions

**RQ1.** Do there exist observable, ex-ante properties of digital-asset firms that predict survival
and continued market access?

**RQ2.** If so, what economic mechanism accounts for their effect — that is, why should these
particular properties matter rather than others?

**RQ3.** Can such properties be operationalised into an instrument that a non-specialist can apply
to a firm from public information?

**RQ4.** Does the framework hold at small scale, where the firm has no balance sheet with which to
purchase the constraints the framework rewards?

## 1.3 The argument in brief

The dissertation's answer to RQ2 supplies the structure for the rest.

Digital-asset markets exhibit adverse selection in an acute form. A counterparty considering whether
to deposit assets with a firm, integrate its product, or hold its liabilities cannot observe whether
that firm segregates customer assets, whether its reserves cover its obligations, or whether its
key management would survive a targeted attack. Honest and dishonest firms make identical claims,
at identical cost, in identical language. Akerlof (1970) established that markets with this
structure degrade toward the lower quality tier, and the sector's history is substantially a record
of that degradation and its periodic violent corrections.

Where quality is unobservable but differentially costly to fake, the resolution is a costly signal
(Spence, 1973; Zahavi, 1975). The dissertation identifies the specific signal that operates in this
market and names it **verifiable restraint**: an irreversible, third-party-checkable narrowing of
the firm's own future action set. Segregating customer assets in a bankruptcy-remote structure means
the firm cannot lend against them during a liquidity squeeze. Publishing a quarterly attestation
means a later shortfall must be disclosed, concealed from an auditor, or explained by ceasing
publication — all detectable. Renouncing ownership of a token contract means no further units can
ever be minted, and the renunciation is a permanent public artifact. Obtaining a licence years
before it is required subjects the firm to supervision it could have avoided.

Each of these is expensive, each forecloses options the firm would otherwise retain, and — critically
— each is *more* expensive for a firm that intends to misbehave than for one that does not. That
asymmetry is what makes the signal separating rather than merely decorative, and it is the
theoretical core of the dissertation, developed in Chapter 3.

## 1.4 Contribution

The dissertation makes four claims to contribution.

**A falsifiable ex-ante construct.** The existing practitioner literature describes well-run firms
after the fact. Verifiable restraint is defined here operationally, in terms of observations that
can be made before the outcome, and each derived proposition in Chapter 7 is stated with the
observation that would falsify it.

**A mechanism, not a list.** The eight propositions are not independent best practices but
consequences of a single underlying trade: the purchase of a floor under adverse outcomes at the
price of defined upside in favourable ones. Chapter 7 shows the eight reduce to that one move, which
is what makes the framework portable to cases the study does not contain — as demonstrated on
prediction markets, a category that scarcely existed when the earlier cases resolved.

**An operational instrument.** Chapter 9 presents a weighted ten-item scorecard derived directly
from the framework, applied to six firms item-by-item so that disagreement can be located on a
specific line rather than argued in aggregate, together with a statement of the instrument's three
known biases.

**A theory-narrowing counter-case.** Chapter 8 treats the observation most damaging to the thesis —
a firm earning roughly $10bn annually while failing the central condition — as a test rather than an
anomaly, and revises the claim accordingly. The revised thesis is weaker than the initial one and is
stated as such.

## 1.5 Scope and delimitations

The study covers firms operating between 2020 and August 2026, with emphasis on the 2024–26 period
in which US federal legislation was enacted and the first substantial cohort of crypto-native firms
completed public listings. It is confined to operating companies. Protocols without a controlling
firm, decentralised autonomous organisations, and pure trading entities are excluded, because the
constructs concern managerial choice and these organisational forms either lack a locus of such
choice or make it unobservable.

The study is qualitative and small-N. It does not produce statistical estimates, and Chapter 13
states plainly what that forecloses.

## 1.6 Structure of the dissertation

Part I establishes the framing: this introduction, a review of the relevant literatures and the gap
between them (Chapter 2), the theoretical framework and derived hypotheses (Chapter 3), and the
research design (Chapter 4). Part II presents findings: the failure base rate (Chapter 5) and eight
case studies (Chapter 6). Part III analyses: eight propositions (Chapter 7), the counter-case
(Chapter 8), and the instrument (Chapter 9). Part IV applies the framework at small scale (Chapters
10–11) and discusses the maximal-score firm and the choice of objective function (Chapter 12). Part
V states limitations (Chapter 13) and concludes (Chapter 14).
