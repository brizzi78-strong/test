# 4. Methodology

## 4.1 Research design

The study uses a theory-building multiple-case design (Eisenhardt, 1989; Yin, 2018). The design is
appropriate on three standard criteria: the phenomenon is contemporary and still unfolding, the
boundary between firm conduct and market context is not clean, and the population of relevant firms
is small enough that statistical designs would be underpowered even with complete data.

Cases are purposively rather than randomly selected, following Flyvbjerg's (2006) argument that
information-rich cases chosen for their theoretical relevance carry more inferential weight than
random draws from a small heterogeneous population. Selection criteria are stated in §4.3, and the
threats this creates are stated in Chapter 13 rather than minimised here.

The unit of analysis is the operating firm. Protocols without a controlling entity are excluded, on
the grounds developed by Aramonte, Huang and Schrimpf (2021): where governance is genuinely
diffuse there is no managerial choice to observe, and where it merely appears diffuse the relevant
choices are made by an identifiable firm that should be studied directly.

## 4.2 The failure-sampling correction

The central methodological hazard in this genre is the one Denrell (2003) identifies: studying
successful organisations and inferring the causes of their success systematically undersamples
failure and produces conclusions that describe the survivors' *variance* rather than their
*advantage*. Any sufficiently long list of things successful firms did will include things
unsuccessful firms also did, invisibly, before failing for unrelated reasons.

Two corrections are applied.

**Failures are analysed for absence, not presence.** Rather than asking what survivors share, the
failure population is examined for which of the four conditions (C1–C4) was violated. This inverts
the inference. The question "what did the dead lack?" admits a short, checkable answer where "what
did the living do?" admits an unbounded one, and Chapter 5 shows the answers cluster tightly on
three mechanisms.

**Hypotheses are stated with falsification conditions.** Each hypothesis in §3.5 and each
proposition in Chapter 7 is accompanied by the observation that would disconfirm it. This does not
eliminate confirmation bias but makes it auditable by a reader who disagrees.

A third correction is attempted and only partially succeeds: Chapter 8 selects the case most damaging
to the thesis and treats it as a test. The thesis is narrowed as a result. This is a weaker
protection than a designed disconfirmation strategy, because the case was chosen by the author from
a population the author already knew.

## 4.3 Case selection

Twenty-four firms were considered; eight receive extended treatment in Chapter 6. Sampling frames:

**Frame 1 — Regulatory-disclosure cohort.** Every crypto-native firm completing a US public listing
in 2025–26 (Circle, Bullish, Gemini, Figure). Listing compels disclosure that private firms may
withhold, so this frame supplies the study's highest-quality evidence and is deliberately
over-represented. §13.2 addresses the resulting bias directly, since a thesis about the value of
disclosure sampled from firms selected for disclosing is close to circular if left unexamined.

**Frame 2 — Private infrastructure.** The largest private infrastructure firms by capital raised
(Fireblocks, Chainalysis, Alchemy, Anchorage, Kraken), included to test whether the framework
survives contact with firms that face no disclosure requirement.

**Frame 3 — Acquisition outcomes.** Bridge and Privy, selected because they repriced the sector's
exit expectations and because both reached those outcomes without issuing a token, making them
critical cases for the token-related propositions.

**Frame 4 — Category replication.** Kalshi and Polymarket, selected as an out-of-sample test:
prediction markets barely existed as a regulated category when the earlier cases resolved, so
reproduction of the pattern there is evidence that the framework is structural rather than an
artifact of one cycle.

**Frame 5 — The counter-case.** Tether, selected specifically because it is the observation most
damaging to the thesis (Chapter 8).

**Frame 6 — Failure control group.** Firms drawn from the 2025–26 closure lists and the 2022 cohort
(FTX, Celsius, Voyager, BlockFi), analysed as specified in §4.2.

## 4.4 Data sources and hierarchy

Evidence is ranked, and the rank is reported at the point of use:

1. **Primary regulatory and corporate documents** — SEC filings, company earnings releases,
   statutory text, government notices (e.g. FBI attribution of the February 2025 Bybit intrusion).
2. **On-chain data** — used where it settles a question reporting does not, and where the artifact
   itself is the evidence (renounce transactions, lock contracts, reserve addresses).
3. **Third-party attestations and audits** — with the distinction between the two treated as
   material rather than terminological (Chapter 8).
4. **Reputable trade and financial press** — used for private-company facts where nothing better
   exists.

Approximately one third of citations fall in category 4. Where a load-bearing claim rests only on
category 4, it is flagged as directional and reported as a range. Private-company valuations are
treated as the weakest evidence in the study; reported figures for the same firm frequently differ
between outlets by margins approaching a factor of two.

## 4.5 Coding

Each firm is coded on four conditions (C1–C4) and subsequently on the ten instrument items of
Chapter 9, each scored 0, 1 or 2 against the rubric anchors specified there. Coding rules and the
full item-by-item results are reproduced in Appendix A so that a reader may recode any firm and
locate disagreement on a specific line.

Two coding conventions warrant statement:

- **Absence of evidence is coded as absence.** A private firm that does not publish an attestation
  is coded 0 on that item, not "unknown". This systematically penalises private firms; §9.4 and
  §13.2 quantify the effect at roughly 10–20 points.
- **Coding is by structure, not intent.** A firm that segregates assets by policy but not by legal
  structure is coded 1, regardless of stated commitment, because the construct concerns what the
  firm *cannot* do rather than what it says it will not do.

Coding was performed by a single analyst. There is no inter-rater reliability statistic, which is a
material limitation (§13.4).

## 4.6 Temporal boundary

Evidence was collected to 15 August 2026. The observation window includes a substantial favourable
shift in US regulation — the GENIUS Act enacted July 2025, the CLARITY Act pending — which
plausibly benefited firms that had adopted restraint early, for reasons partly independent of the
mechanism proposed here. Each proposition in Chapter 7 carries an explicit regulatory-timing
exposure rating, and §13.3 treats this as the second most serious threat to validity.
