## The Question in the Hallway

I carried the pitch in my bag for a year and a half. I knew it cold. The clinical data. The A1C reductions. The once-a-week dosing. The objection handlers, in order, like wrestling drills. I could deliver the whole thing in ninety seconds in a hallway while a doctor walked away from me.

Most days, that's exactly what selling Ozempic looked like. A hallway. A walking doctor. Ninety seconds.

Then one Tuesday I stopped doing it.

Family practice office in New Jersey, fall of 2019. I'd been calling on this doctor for months. He was always behind. Fifteen-minute slots, twenty-six patients, a waiting room full of people holding their coats on their laps. He came around the corner, saw me, and put his hand up before I said a word.

"I don't have time for a pitch."

"Okay," I said. "No pitch. One question. Who's the patient you're worried about?"

He stopped walking.

He looked at me like nobody had asked him that all year. Then he leaned against the wall and looked at the floor.

"There's a woman," he said. "Nine years diabetic and her numbers keep climbing. She won't start insulin. It's the needles. And it's not the needles. Her mother had it too. Went blind before she died. She watched her mother take the shots the whole way down. So to her the needle isn't medicine. It's the first day of the end."

Every visit, she sat on his exam table and apologized to him.

"She thinks she's letting me down," he said. "She cries in the parking lot. My MA sees her out there."

Everyone in that building knew. She still cried alone.

I didn't open my bag.

"Once a week," I said. "A needle thinner than an eyelash — half the patients say they can't feel it. I'll walk your staff through the prior auth. I know which boxes her plan kicks back. If they say no, I'll have the savings card and a template appeal letter here the same day. That part's on us, not her."

He signed for the samples on my iPad without my asking. Then he went back to his twenty-six patients, and I drove to the next office.

Three months later I was standing in the same hallway. He came around the corner and this time he was looking for me.

"She came in Monday," he said. "Rough first month — she called the office twice about the nausea. But she stayed on it. First A1C in nine years that started with a seven."

He paused. Twenty-six patients a day, and his voice caught.

"After you left that day, I kept thinking about the parking lot. So this visit, before she could start, I told her: you have never once let me down. You have a disease." He looked at the floor again. "She started to apologize anyway. Nine years of habit. I watched her catch herself and stop."

Neither of us said anything for a second. Then his nurse called him, and he went.

There's a plaque in my garage somewhere with my name on it from that year. A ranking. A bonus check I couldn't tell you the amount of. What I remember is a woman I never met. I never learned her name. And here's the uncomfortable part: the question worked. It opened doors, the doors opened prescriptions, and the prescriptions paid my mortgage. I still can't tell you what I was doing in that hallway — caring, or closing. Maybe that Tuesday it was both.

Years later I stopped carrying the bag. Home health first, then hospice. Rooms with nothing to sell and nothing to close.

The hallway taught me the question. It didn't teach me the rest. That Tuesday, after I asked it, I got in my car and drove to the next office. That was the job then: ask, and leave.

Hospice taught me what comes after. I still ask the question. Now I stay for the answer.

## Samples, No Credit

My paycheck was a GLP-1. My trunk was full of insulin.

That's how sampling worked. The bag carried what the company made, and the company made insulin — had made it for almost a century. But my number, the one on the list, the one that decided the plaque and the bonus, only moved when doctors wrote the drug I was assigned. The insulin samples were just something I stocked. No quota. No credit. Nothing in the dashboard changed when I dropped them off.

Which would be a boring detail about sales-force compensation, except for what was happening to insulin in America while I was driving it around.

The list price had gone up and up until a vial cost hundreds of dollars, and by then the news had a word for what people were doing about it: rationing. Stretching doses. Skipping days. There were people dying of a drug that existed in warehouses. Dying of the price. Congress held hearings. Eventually the prices came down. But in the years I'm telling you about, the fix hadn't come yet, and the gap between the warehouse and the patient was being bridged, quietly, all over the country, by one thing.

The sample closet.

Every office had one. A cabinet, a mini-fridge, a shelf the MA guarded like a vault. And in office after office, there were patients — the nurses knew exactly who — living month to month on what was in it. Not officially. There's no program called that. Just a nurse keeping a mental list, a doctor writing "samples" in a chart, and a rep who kept showing up with a trunk.

So here is what my week actually looked like, some weeks. The calls that fed my number, the hallways, the question, the prior auths — that was the job. And then the stops that fed nothing: the office on the county road that always, always asked if I had insulin with me. The nurse who met me in the parking lot once because a patient was in the waiting room down to his last units and she didn't want him to hear her asking. She took the box, said the doctor would sign, and walked back in through the front so it looked like nothing. I signed the forms. The doctor signed the forms. Nothing about it ever appeared anywhere I was measured.

The forms mattered more than you'd think. There was a phrase from training that lived in every rep's head: *sample prison.* Of everything in my life those years — the quota, the ranking, the money — the samples were the only thing that could put me in a federal courtroom. There's a law about them, older than my career, written back when reps really did sell samples out of trunks, and it has teeth. Every box counted. Every drop-off signed. Audits where a man with a clipboard reconciled my trunk against my paperwork like I was a bank branch. Forge a signature to save a Friday afternoon, and training was blunt about where that road ends. We half-joked about it the way you half-joke about anything with teeth.

The drug that made me money could never hurt me. The drug that made me nothing was keeping people alive — and it was the one thing in the car that could take my freedom. I signed everything twice.

The company made the insulin and let us give it away by the trunkload — that was them. The doctor took the liability. The nurse kept the list. All I did was drive it there instead of leaving it in the trunk. Most reps I knew did the same. I never asked about the offices nobody drove to. It cost me nothing but weight in the car.

Every week, the dashboard told me what I was worth: one number. And every week there was this other ledger — the county-road office, the nurse in the parking lot, the closet — that no dashboard anywhere was keeping. The most important thing in my trunk was the thing I got no credit for.

## The Last Refill

There is a part of a hospice admission that nobody warns families about: the medication list.

A nurse sits down with the daughter or the husband or whoever keeps the pills organized, and they go through it together, line by line, and some of the lines get gently retired. The statin that was protecting a heart against a future that has changed shape. The blood pressure pill nobody needs to stand up slowly for anymore. The vitamins.

And then, in the last few years, again and again, the list arrives at a name I used to say for a living.

I was in the room the first time it happened in front of me. I'd brought the referral in, and the family had asked me to stay for the admission. A kitchen table, a daughter with her father's medications lined up in front of her like a defense. She'd fought for some of those. When the list reached the pen in the refrigerator door — the one I used to carry in a bag — the daughter put her hand flat on the table.

"That one works," she said. "Do you know what we went through to get that one?"

I did, as it happens.

The prior authorization. The appeal. The pharmacy calling around during the shortage. The month of nausea her father pushed through. Years of copays. She'd fought for every line of it.

The nurse didn't argue. She asked what the drug had been for.

"His numbers," the daughter said. "His A1C. It got him to a seven."

"Where he is now," the nurse said, "we're not treating the numbers anymore. We're treating days. And this one" — she touched the box gently, like it deserved respect — "works by taking away appetite. Right now, appetite is precious. If your dad wants ice cream at ten in the morning, we want every bite of that to be wonderful. I'm going to recommend to our doctor that we let this one go."

The daughter sat there a second. Then, quietly: "If we stop it, I'm giving up on him."

"You're not giving up on him," the nurse said. "You're the reason he's home. Nothing we stop today stops you taking care of him."

She didn't rush past it. She let the daughter cry before the next line on the list.

When there was space, I said the one thing the nurse couldn't. I told her I used to sell that drug. That it was a good drug, and it had done its job — it got him here, to this kitchen, to her. That this part wasn't its job. This part was hers. I don't remember my exact words.

I remember the ice cream, later — a few visits on. Butter pecan, ten in the morning, her father eating it slowly at that same table, the defense of pill bottles cleared away. The daughter watched every spoonful. She didn't say anything.

Neither did I.
