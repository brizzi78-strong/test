## Overview

Before Ozempic was a punchline, a shortage, and a cultural revolution, it was a pen in a bag in the trunk of a car in New Jersey — and Rob Brizzi carried the bag.

He was hired to sell diabetes care without knowing the difference between type 1 and type 2 diabetes. He learned the disease off flashcards, learned the territory in fifteen-minute hallway conversations, and learned the job's real secret by accident: the question that moved more medicine than any pitch ever written — *"Who's the patient you're worried about?"* He rode that question to the number one ranking in the nation, a plaque, a bonus, and a night when his company closed Universal Studios so a few thousand salespeople could ride roller coasters in an empty park.

Then the drug got famous, and Rob watched from the outside as everything he'd sold changed meaning: the shortage that emptied his wife's pharmacy shelves (Hope is a PharmD — this book's second set of eyes), the compounding Wild West, the med-spa vials, the monologue jokes, the headlines that scared patients off medicine that was working. And he watched from the inside of a different job — hospice — where he'd landed after the bag, and where one day, at a kitchen-table admission, a nurse's medication review reached the pen in the refrigerator door: the drug a family had fought prior authorizations, shortages, and a month of nausea to get, gently retired so a dying man could enjoy butter pecan ice cream at ten in the morning.

*The Other Ledger* is the first inside account of the biggest drug story of this generation — and it is not a business book. It is a book about the two ledgers every salesperson, every clinician, and every family keeps: the one with numbers on it, and the one that counts. Rob's dashboard measured one drug, two weeks late. The other ledger — the insulin samples he stocked in closets for no credit while Americans rationed; the nurse who met him in a parking lot so a patient wouldn't hear her asking; the woman who stopped apologizing to her doctor — was the real story all along. It took a hospice job to teach him how to read it.

**One sentence:** *Hard Sell* meets *Being Mortal* — the Ozempic years told by the only kind of witness who hasn't spoken yet: the man who sold it, married to the woman who dispensed it, now working where it gets let go.

## Audience

The tens of millions of Americans on (or considering, or priced out of) GLP-1s; the diabetes community and their families; pharmacists, nurses, and reps (the "help desk" readers who live these chapters); hospice and caregiving readers arriving from Book One; book clubs that made *Being Mortal* and *Dopesick* perennials.

## Comparable Titles

- **Hard Sell** (Jamie Reidy) — the direct precedent: insider rep memoir, film adaptation; ours trades its cynicism for stakes (insulin rationing, hospice) the Viagra story never had.
- **Magic Pill** (Johann Hari, 2024) — proof of an Ozempic book market; a journalist's inquiry, where ours is testimony.
- **Dopesick** (Beth Macy) — the pharma-rep moral machinery, from outside; ours is the view from inside the machinery, about a drug that (unlike opioids) mostly worked.
- **Being Mortal** (Atul Gawande) — the medicine-meets-mortality register of Part III, for the reader who wants meaning, not exposé.
