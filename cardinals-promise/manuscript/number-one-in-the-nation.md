There is a list, in that business, and everyone knows where they are on it.

They don't hand it to you on paper. It arrives in dashboards and district calls and the tone your manager uses in October. You can feel your number the way you can feel weather. I had been climbing it for years — top of the district, top of the region, close-but at the national meeting while somebody else walked across the stage.

Then one year the list ran out of names above mine.

Number one in the nation. Out of everybody, carrying the same bag, saying the same words, working the same hallways. Me. The kid from New Jersey who started this job in a parking lot, looking up the disease on his phone.

I'd like to tell you I took it in stride. I didn't. I wanted it the way I had wanted my hand raised on a mat — wanted it in my body, since long before pharma, since the wrestling room, since before I understood what I was actually hungry for. When they said my name, the roar in that ballroom was for me, and I stood there inside it and thought: *so this is the top of the list.*

And here is the part I have never fully explained to anyone, the part this book exists to explain.

What got me there was the question. Not the closes, not the ninety-second pitch — I had those, everyone had those. The number came from years of hallways where I put the pitch away and asked doctors who they were worried about, and then did the unglamorous paperwork of caring: the prior auth walked through, the template letter delivered same-day, the follow-up three months later. The industry's own scoreboard, built to measure selling, had accidentally measured showing up.

So the plaque says one thing and means another, and I'm the only one who can read it.

I called Lou from the hallway outside the ballroom, awards music bleeding through the doors. My father answered the way he always answered. What passed between us in that phone call belongs to him, and this book will get to him — every road in it does. I'll say this much: I have had my hand raised twice in rooms full of people, once on a mat at seventeen and once in that ballroom, and both times the first thing I did was look for Lou.

The next morning the list reset to zero, the way lists do. New year, new quota, the same hallways. Whatever the top of the list is, you rent it. You never own it.

Years later I took a job with no list at all. Nobody ranks hospice visits. There's no ballroom, no music, nobody's name on a screen. For a while, honestly, I missed it — you don't spend a life chasing raised hands and stop needing them because the work got holy.

Then it left me, quietly, the way it does when a family holds the door of a room and says: he waited for you. That's the whole leaderboard now.

Number one in the nation is the thing I say when people ask how I did in pharma.

*He waited for you* is the thing I'd carve on the plaque.
