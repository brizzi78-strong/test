# Diabetes Dave

*The pharma years — the man who taught me what the disease actually costs.*

Everybody called him Diabetes Dave.

It was half a joke the way nicknames are, and like most nicknames it was also just the truth. He knew the disease better than anyone I ever worked with. Not sales-knowledge. Not slide-deck knowledge. He knew it the way you know a road you've driven at night in the rain.

I worked alongside him in the years I was learning the difference between type 1 and type 2 off flashcards, and for a while I thought that was all it was — a guy who'd been in the business long enough to know everything.

Then someone told me about his son.

Dave had a son. He was twenty-one when he died.

I'm not going to write here what I didn't witness, and I'm not going to dress the fact up, because the fact does not need me. A man I worked with buried his twenty-one-year-old boy, and then he got up, and he spent his working life inside the disease that took him.

Once I knew, I couldn't unsee it. It was in everything he did. Why he never rushed a question about a patient. Why the science mattered to him like scripture — why he'd correct a detail the rest of us would let slide, because somewhere out there was a kid whose life might sit on that detail. Why he had time for the newest, greenest rep asking the dumbest question at the end of the longest day. I was that rep. He never once made me feel like my not-knowing was a problem.

He'd already paid the full price of the thing we were all just employed by. And what he did with that price was stay. In the industry. In the rooms. In the details. Twenty-some years before I ever sat with a hospice family, Diabetes Dave was showing me the whole job and I didn't know it yet: the worst thing happens, and you don't leave. You let it turn into the reason you're good to people.

I think about the fathers I sit with now, in the worst weeks of their lives, and how some of them ask me — not in these words — what they're supposed to do with the rest of it. The grief. The time. Themselves.

I never know what to tell them. But I know what one man did.

He became Diabetes Dave.
