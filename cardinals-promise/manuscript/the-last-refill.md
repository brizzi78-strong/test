*Hospice — the day the drug everyone fought for gets gently let go.*

There is a part of a hospice admission that nobody warns families about: the medication list.

A nurse sits down with the daughter or the husband or whoever keeps the pills organized, and they go through it together, line by line, and some of the lines get gently retired. The statin that was protecting a heart against a future that has changed shape. The blood pressure pill nobody needs to stand up slowly for anymore. The vitamins.

And then, in the last few years, again and again, the list arrives at a name I used to say for a living.

I was in the room the first time it happened in front of me. An admission visit, a kitchen table, a daughter with her father's medications lined up in front of her like a defense. She'd fought for some of those. When the nurse's pen reached the pen — the one in the refrigerator door, the one I used to carry in a bag — the daughter put her hand flat on the table.

"That one works," she said. "Do you know what we went through to get that one?"

I did, as it happens. Better than almost anyone in any room she'd ever be in.

Because here's what "that one" costs a family: the prior authorization. The appeal. The pharmacy calling around during the shortage. The month of nausea her father pushed through. Years of copays. That drug wasn't a medication to her. It was a war she'd won on her father's behalf, and this nurse wanted to surrender it.

The nurse didn't argue. She asked what the drug had been for.

"His numbers," the daughter said. "His A1C. It got him to a seven."

"Where he is now," the nurse said, "we're not treating the numbers anymore. We're treating days. And this one" — she touched the box gently, like it deserved respect — "works by taking away appetite. Right now, appetite is precious. If your dad wants ice cream at ten in the morning, we want every bite of that to be wonderful."

The daughter sat there a second. Then she said the thing under the thing, the sentence the whole visit was actually about:

"If we stop it, I'm giving up on him."

There it was. The unspoken words in that kitchen were never about semaglutide.

The nurse handled it the way the good ones do, and I'll spend the rest of my life learning to do it that well. But there was one thing in the room only I could say, so when there was space, I said it.

"I used to sell that drug. It's a good drug. It did its job — it got him here, to this kitchen, to you. This part isn't its job. This part is yours."

I don't know that I said it that cleanly. You never do, in the moment. But I remember the ice cream, later — the actual ice cream, a few visits on. Butter pecan, ten in the morning, her father eating it slowly at that same table with the defense of pill bottles cleared away, and the daughter watching him like the win she'd actually been fighting for all along had finally arrived.

Forty years of watching his numbers. He got to spend his last summer tasting things.

I sold the future for a living, and it was honest work; the future is where most people live. Hospice is where I learned there's a season when love stops managing the future and starts serving the day. The drug was for the years ahead. The ice cream was for the morning it was eaten in.

Both were love. You just have to know what time it is.
