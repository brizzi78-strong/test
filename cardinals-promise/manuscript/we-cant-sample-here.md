Let me be clear about something: it was never supposed to be a weight-loss drug.

It was a diabetes drug. Approved for diabetes. I was trained within an inch of my life to say that and nothing else. The weight-loss version — Wegovy, same molecule, its own label, its own dose — went into shortage almost as soon as it existed and stayed there. The country wanted one thing. The label said another.

I stayed on label.

Because the craze didn't come to me through a monologue or a hashtag. It came through the sample closet.

Offices I'd called on for years started greeting me differently. Not "who's the patient you're worried about." Just: *we need samples.* Again at the next visit: *we need samples.* A hunger in it. Offices that used to take a box were asking for cases.

And it wasn't only staff. I was at a lunch with my boss when a doctor said, give me one of those pens — one he'd just signed for. Then he lifted his shirt and injected himself at the table. Signed for, legal, his. My boss and I looked at each other and said nothing. There was nothing to say.

And I was the guy who counted. That was the deal with samples — every box logged, every signature collected, and a law with teeth waiting behind the paperwork. So I did what the counting man does. I looked at the numbers.

The scripts weren't there.

An office draining samples is supposed to show up, two weeks later, as new prescriptions — new patients started, titrated, converted to the pharmacy. That's what samples are *for*. This office was going through product far past anything its size explained, and the script data was flat.

I sat in my car in their parking lot with the call log open and did the only math available. The same hunger that was emptying that closet was making my number the best it had ever been.

And then I started actually looking at the people who buzzed me through the door. The front desk. The back office. And I kept looking at the numbers and saying the only sentence the numbers left me:

There weren't any new scripts, and the staff was getting skinnier.

The samples weren't reaching patients. They were walking down the hall. I never saw a box leave. I couldn't prove a thing. I could count.

They stood closer to that drug than almost anyone in America — watching patients transform, hearing the hype, wanting what everyone wanted, with a closet of it twenty feet away and the version they could legally be prescribed locked in a shortage. And I had to keep walking that hallway.

Somewhere in that office's panel were the people the closet existed for — the ones rationing insulin down to their last units in another office on my route. And every box that walked was signed for under a law with my name on the form.

So I made the call that ended a relationship I'd spent years building.

We can't sample here anymore.
