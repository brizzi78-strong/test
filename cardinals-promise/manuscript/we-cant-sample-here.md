Let me be clear about something, because I was there: it was never supposed to be a weight-loss drug.

Not the one I carried. The weight came off some patients — everybody saw it, the trials had seen it — but Ozempic was a diabetes drug, approved for diabetes, and I was trained within an inch of my life to say exactly that and nothing else. When the weight-loss version came — Wegovy, same molecule, its own label, its own dose — it promptly went into shortage and stayed there. So for a long stretch of the famous years, the country wanted one thing, the label said another, and in between stood every rep in America, staying on label while the culture screamed off it.

I stayed on label. I want that on the record not because it makes me good, but because of what it cost to watch.

Because the craze didn't come to me through a monologue or a hashtag. It came through the sample closet.

Offices I'd called on for years started greeting me differently. Not "who's the patient you're worried about." Just: *we need samples.* Again at the next visit: *we need samples.* A hunger in it. Offices that used to take a box were asking for cases.

And I was the guy who counted. That was the deal with samples — every box logged, every signature collected, my trunk reconciled against my paperwork like a bank branch. So I did what the counting man does. I looked at the numbers.

The scripts weren't there.

An office draining samples is supposed to show up, two weeks later, as new prescriptions — new patients started, titrated, converted to the pharmacy. That's what samples are *for*. This office was going through product like a clinic twice its size, and the script data was flat. Nothing new. Nowhere.

I sat in my car in their parking lot with the call log open and did the only math available.

And then I started actually looking at the people who buzzed me through the door. The front desk. The MA. The office manager who always signed. And I'm going to say it the way I said it then, because any softer version is a lie:

There weren't any new scripts, and the staff was getting skinnier.

The samples weren't reaching patients. They were walking down the hall.

I want to be careful about the people in that office, because I understood them. They stood closer to that drug than almost anyone in America — watching patients transform, hearing the hype, wanting what everyone wanted, with a closet of it twenty feet away and a version they could actually be prescribed locked in a shortage. They weren't cartoon villains. They were the craze, concentrated into one hallway I had to walk every other week.

But those samples had patients' names on them in every way that mattered. Somewhere in that office's panel were the people the closet existed for — the ones rationing a different drug in a different chapter of this book. And every box that walked down the hall was signed for under a law with my name on the form and teeth I'd been warned about since training.

So I made the call that ended a relationship I'd spent years building.

We can't sample here anymore.
