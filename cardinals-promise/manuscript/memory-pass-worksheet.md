# Memory-Pass Worksheet — Certifying the Sample Chapters

*The #1 item on the agent's convert-to-offer list: the three sample
chapters must contain only lived scenes before they attach to the proposal.
This worksheet lists every stand-in in those chapters as a question. Answer
in your own words — voice notes, fragments, misspellings all fine; the
drafts get rebuilt from your answers, then you sign off. If a drafted
moment never happened and nothing like it did, say "cut it" — an honest
hole beats a borrowed scene.*

**Rule for every answer:** where were you, who was there, what was said
(exact words if you have them), what did your body do, what didn't you say?

---

## Chapter: The Question in the Hallway

1. The office: what town, what kind of practice, roughly what year? What do
   you remember seeing from that hallway?
2. The doctor: real person (name withheld/changed) or a blend? What did he
   actually look like / do when you asked the question?
3. **Your opening question — the exact words you used** when you dropped
   the pitch. (The drafted "Who's the patient you're worried about?" — is
   that your sentence or my guess?)
4. The patient he told you about: what do you actually remember of her
   story? (The insulin fear, the mother who went blind, the parking-lot
   crying, the apologizing — which of these are real, which are drafted?)
5. What did YOU say next — really? (The drafted needle line, the prior-auth
   offer, the "That part's on us, not her.")
6. The return visit: how much later, and what did he actually tell you?
   (The A1C starting with a seven; the nausea calls; "she didn't apologize."
   The doctor saying the unspoken words — "you have never once let me
   down" — is the panel's proposal, NOT a memory. Did anything like it
   happen? If not: cut, and tell me what did.)
7. The plaque/garage detail: what do you actually have from that year, and
   where is it?

## Chapter: Samples, No Credit

1. Which insulins rode in your trunk, and what was true about the credit —
   uncomped for you, comped to a counterpart, or something else?
2. The county-road office: was there a real office that always asked? Where?
   Who asked?
3. **The parking-lot nurse** — did this happen? If yes: where, when,
   what did she say, what did you do? If it was a different moment that
   shows the same thing, give me that one instead.
4. Sample prison: the exact phrase as your trainers used it, one audit you
   actually sat through, and whether "I signed everything twice" is
   literal.
5. The rationing years from your seat: what did you actually see or hear in
   offices about patients who couldn't afford insulin? One real moment.
6. "Most reps I knew did the same" — true as written?

## Chapter: The Last Refill

1. Were you ever actually in the room when a hospice medication review
   reached a GLP-1? If yes: everything you remember. If no: what's the
   closest real moment (a family's question to you, a nurse's story, an
   IDG discussion)? The chapter rebuilds from whatever is true.
2. Why were you there — what's the real version of "brought the referral
   in, asked to stay"?
3. Your beat — did you ever tell a family some version of "I used to sell
   that drug; it did its job; this part is yours"? Exact memory, or cut.
4. The ice cream: real detail from a real patient (changed details), or
   drafted? What's YOUR butter-pecan detail — the one you actually
   witnessed?
5. The daughter's "If we stop it, I'm giving up on him" — you've heard
   families say this. Give me one real time (who, where, what the nurse
   actually answered).

---

## Sign-off protocol

For each chapter, when the rebuild from your answers is done, you read it
aloud once and confirm three things in writing: (1) every scene happened or
is explicitly framed as composite in the text itself; (2) every quoted line
is remembered speech or clearly marked gist; (3) you'd defend every detail
to a fact-checker with a straight face. Then — and only then — it's a
sample chapter.
