# The Pen and the Pill

*The pharma years — Ozempic and Rybelsus: same molecule, two doors.*

Almost two years into carrying Ozempic, they handed me the same drug in a different shape.

Rybelsus. The first GLP-1 you could swallow. Same molecule I'd been selling in a pen, now in a tablet — which sounds like a small thing and was, at the time, close to a miracle of chemistry. Proteins like that aren't supposed to survive a stomach. The company had spent years teaching one to. The trick barely worked — almost none of the dose survived the trip — which is why the pill came with rules.

I thought it would sell itself. A pill instead of a needle, in a disease full of people afraid of needles? I'd been in the hallways. I knew what the needle cost people. Here was the other door.

Then I read the fine print I'd be spelling out for the rest of my run.

The pill had rules. Empty stomach. First thing in the morning. No more than four ounces of plain water — not coffee, not juice, four ounces, plain. Then nothing — no food, no coffee, none of your other pills — for thirty minutes. Every morning, forever.

Try that sentence out on an actual life. A woman who takes six other medications with breakfast. A man who drives a truck at four a.m. with a thermos of coffee riding shotgun. A mother whose mornings are two kids and a bus schedule. The pill was easier to take and harder to live with. The pen was scarier to start and easier to keep. You couldn't know which without knowing their mornings.

So that's what the job became, in the offices where they let me: asking doctors not "which one do you like" but "what do her mornings look like?" The wrong one ends up in a drawer. Full price, thirty pills, untouched.

There was a doctor ready to switch a needle-shy patient to the pill — obvious call, on paper. I asked what her mornings looked like; it was the only question I had by then. It came out that she took her mother to dialysis at six a.m. three days a week and ate breakfast in the car. He sat there for a second.

"So the pill's the harder one for her."

On paper, the pill was the easy version. In her life, the pen was. Ten seconds, once a week, any time of day. She could be afraid of it once a week instead of organized for it every morning.

Whose mornings are these? Hers.
