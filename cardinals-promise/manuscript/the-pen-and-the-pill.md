# The Pen and the Pill

*The pharma years — Ozempic and Rybelsus: same molecule, two doors.*

A year into carrying Ozempic, they handed me the same drug in a different shape.

Rybelsus. The first GLP-1 you could swallow. Same molecule I'd been selling in a pen, now in a tablet — which sounds like a small thing and was, at the time, close to a miracle of chemistry. Proteins like that aren't supposed to survive a stomach. The company had spent years teaching one to.

I thought it would sell itself. A pill instead of a needle, in a disease full of people afraid of needles? I'd been in the hallways. I knew what the needle cost people. Here was the other door.

Then I read the fine print I'd be spelling out for the rest of my run.

The pill had rules. Empty stomach. First thing in the morning. No more than four ounces of plain water — not coffee, not juice, four ounces, plain. Then nothing — no food, no coffee, none of your other pills — for thirty minutes. Every morning, forever.

Try that sentence out on an actual life. A woman who takes six other medications with breakfast. A man who drives a truck at four a.m. with a thermos of coffee riding shotgun. A mother whose mornings are two kids and a bus schedule. The pill was easier to take and harder to live with. The pen was scarier to start and easier to keep. Neither one was better. They were better *for somebody* — and you couldn't know who without knowing their mornings.

So that's what the job became, in the offices where they let me: asking doctors not "which one do you like" but "what do her mornings look like?" A drug that fits the life gets taken. A drug that fights the life ends up in a drawer, at full price, doing nothing but generating guilt every time the drawer opens.

I watched a doctor figure this out in real time once. He'd been ready to switch a needle-shy patient to the pill — obvious call, on paper. Then somebody asked about her mornings, and it came out that she took her mother to dialysis at six a.m. three days a week and ate breakfast in the car. He sat there for a second. Then he said, "So the pill's the harder one for her," like the sentence surprised him on its way out.

On paper, the pill was the easy version. In her life, the pen was. Ten seconds, once a week, any time of day. She could be afraid of it once a week instead of organized for it every morning.

I don't know how her story went either — by then you know that about me. But I kept the lesson in the bag long after I turned the bag in. In hospice nobody hands you two versions of a molecule. But every plan we make — where the bed goes, who sleeps when, what gets said and what waits — is the same question wearing different clothes.

Not "which one is best."

Whose mornings are these?
