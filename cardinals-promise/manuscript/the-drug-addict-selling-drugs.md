Here is the sentence I have been circling for this whole book.

I'm a drug addict, and I spent my career selling drugs.

I know the objections. Pharmaceuticals aren't street drugs; a rep with a detail bag isn't a dealer; the medicine I carried saved lives and I believe that today more than ever. All true. The sentence stays anyway, because it was true first. I got sober on January 9, 2009. [Rob — the road to that date is yours to tell or not tell. The draft leaves it silent on purpose. If it belongs in this book, write it; if it belongs in the other book, point there; if it belongs nowhere, silence is honest too.] And some years later, a man in recovery walked into a pharmaceutical company and said, in effect: I'm very good at getting people to want things. And they hired him, and they were right.

I want to tell you what an addict knows that no training class teaches.

An addict knows craving from the inside. Not as a concept — as a tenant. So when a man in a waiting room apologized to a clipboard for being "bad this month," I didn't need the psychology explained. I had said those words. Different counter, different substance, same sentence. When a woman feared that starting insulin meant she had failed, I recognized the arithmetic, because it's addict arithmetic: the substance becomes the scoreboard, and the scoreboard becomes your worth. Everything this book says about shame, I learned before training camp. Novo Nordisk taught me the pancreas. The disease of wanting, I brought with me.

[Rob — the slot that matters most: did the two ever touch in the field? A moment when the sobriety and the job looked at each other — a doctor's offhand comment, a pharma dinner with the wine flowing, a sample-closet thought, a meeting you drove to after a sales call. One true moment carries this chapter; without one, the chapter stays an essay and should shrink.]

And then the story bent in a direction nobody saw coming, least of all me.

The drug I sold started showing up in addiction research. Studies — real ones, placebo-controlled — began reporting that semaglutide quiets alcohol craving. Heavy-drinking days down. The wanting itself, dialed down by the same molecule I used to carry in a bag. They're running bigger trials now. The molecule I sold for blood sugar may end up standing in the doorway of my own disease.

I don't know what to do with that yet. That's not a rhetorical device; I actually don't. Sobriety, the way I got it, was not a molecule. It was [Rob — yours: the program, the people, the daily practice — say it your way or leave it unsaid]. Seventeen years of it, one day at a time, no injection involved. So when someone asks me — and they will — whether I'd have taken a craving drug in 2009 if it had existed, I owe the question a real answer, and I don't have one I trust. Some days I think it would have saved me years. Some days I think the wanting had to be walked through, not muted. Both of those might be true. Recovery taught me to live with sentences like that.

What I know is this. Somewhere out there is a person at their own kitchen counter, in the hour before they decide, doing the math I used to do. If a pen in a refrigerator door can change that hour — the way one changed a woman's nine years of apologizing — then I hope the studies are right, and I hope somebody shows up alongside the prescription. Because the drug, whichever drug, was never the whole answer.

Somebody stayed with me. That was the medicine.
