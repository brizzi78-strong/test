# The Shortage

*A chapter from the pharma years — when the drug I used to sell stopped being findable.*

Hope came home late on a Thursday and didn't take her coat off. She sat down at the kitchen table with it still zipped and said, "I told eleven people no today."

My wife is a pharmacist. She has a doctorate in the exact distance between what a medication can do and what a family can get. For most of her career, "no" meant something fixable — a prior authorization, a cheaper generic, a phone call to the prescriber. She is very good at turning no into not-yet.

That year, no meant no. Nothing in the fridge. Nothing on the truck. Nothing in the system, which she could see and the patient couldn't, which meant every day somebody decided she was lying.

The drug was semaglutide. Ozempic. The one I used to carry in a bag.

---

I spent years of my life getting that drug to people. I knew its lot numbers and its cold chain and its copay cards. I sat in hallways and asked doctors who they were worried about, and the answer was always somebody like the woman with nine years of climbing numbers, and the hard part — the whole job — was getting the system to say yes to her.

Then the drug got famous, and the hard part became the factory.

You know this story even if you think you don't. Around 2022 the internet discovered that the diabetes drug made people lose weight, and demand went vertical. Not doubled — vertical. The company I used to work for was building plants as fast as concrete sets and it didn't matter. The FDA put it on the shortage list. And in a shortage, nobody decides who gets the last pen in the fridge. Everybody decides. Which means the lottery lands on whoever happens to be standing at the counter on the wrong Thursday.

It would be easy to write a villain into this chapter. I tried. The woman taking it to lose forty pounds her doctor had been begging her to lose — she wasn't a villain. Obesity was the diagnosis on her chart, and the drug was approved for exactly that, and her forty pounds were going to become my old customers' heart failure eventually. The med-spa crowd, the vanity scripts — easier to sneer at, but I sold pharmaceuticals for a living; I am not the man who gets to sneer at wanting. The company wasn't a villain; you can't wish a factory into existence. Even the insurers, my oldest sparring partners, were doing arithmetic somebody was always going to have to do.

There was no villain. There was just not enough. And "not enough" doesn't distribute itself by need. It distributes itself by luck, and money, and who has a pharmacist in the family.

---

Here is what a shortage looks like from the kitchen table of a pharmacist.

It looks like Hope on the phone at nine at night, off the clock, calling the store two towns over because a man with an A1C of eleven had been to four pharmacies and hers was the first one where somebody looked past the screen. It looks like her teaching a scared woman, in the ten feet between the counter and the door, how to make the last pen stretch safely until the truck came — because the woman was going to stretch it anyway, and the only question was whether anyone showed her how.

It looks like absorbing anger all day that belongs to a supply chain, because the supply chain doesn't have a face and she does.

And it looks like the sentence she said that Thursday, coat still zipped, which I have not been able to put down since:

"The ones who scare me aren't the ones who yell. It's the ones who say 'okay' and walk out. Because I don't know what they go home and do."

Some of them went home and did nothing, and their numbers climbed. Some went back on insulin — and if you read the last chapter, you know what going back to the needle meant for some of them. It meant the thing they had finally stopped being afraid of was gone, and the thing they feared was back, and no one in the whole apparatus was assigned to say to them: *this is not your fault. You didn't fail. The system ran out, not you.*

Nobody's job. So mostly, nobody said it.

Hope said it. Eleven times that day, I'd bet, in her own words, in the ten feet she had. She couldn't make the drug exist. What she had was the walk between the counter and the door, and she used it.

I keep learning this same lesson in different rooms. You almost never get to fix the thing. The factory is not yours. The formulary is not yours. The diagnosis is not yours. What's yours is the ten feet, and whether you spend them looking at the screen or at the person.

The shortage ended, eventually, the way shortages do — new plants, new supply, a quiet announcement in early 2025, and everyone stopped talking about it.

At our house we didn't, quite. Some Thursdays she still counts the day in people.
