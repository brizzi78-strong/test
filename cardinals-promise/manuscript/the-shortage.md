# The Shortage

*A chapter from the pharma years — when the drug I used to sell stopped being findable.*

Hope came home late on a Thursday and didn't take her coat off. She sat down at the kitchen table with it still zipped and said, "I told eleven people no today."

My wife is a pharmacist. For most of her career, no meant something fixable — a prior authorization, a cheaper generic, a phone call to the prescriber. She is very good at turning no into not-yet.

That year, no meant no. Nothing in the fridge. Nothing on the truck. Nothing in the system, which she could see and the patient couldn't, which meant every day somebody decided she was lying.

The drug was semaglutide. Ozempic. The one I used to carry in a bag.

By then I was in hospice rooms all day. The drug found its way home to me anyway, at my own kitchen table.

---

I spent years of my life getting that drug to people. I knew its lot numbers and its cold chain and its copay cards. I sat in hallways and asked doctors who they were worried about, and the answer was always somebody like the woman with nine years of climbing numbers, and the hard part was getting the system to say yes to her.

Then the drug got famous, and the hard part became the factory.

Around 2022 the internet found out the diabetes drug made people lose weight. After that there was never enough of it again. The company I used to work for was building plants as fast as concrete sets and it didn't matter. The FDA put it on the shortage list. And in a shortage, nobody is in charge of who gets the last pen in the fridge — not by need, anyway. The company allocated. The wholesalers allocated. The chains allocated. And after all that arithmetic, it still came down to whoever was standing at the counter on the wrong Thursday.

The woman taking it for the forty pounds her doctor had been begging her about — she'd been losing and regaining that weight since high school, and for the first time in her life the number was moving and staying moved. The version approved for exactly that is called Wegovy — same molecule, different box — and it was even harder to find, so doctors wrote Ozempic instead, and the shortage ate its own tail. She wasn't a villain. She was a patient. Even the ones taking it for reasons I'd once have rolled my eyes at — I sold want for a living. I'm the last man alive who gets to grade anybody's reasons.

There was no villain. There was just not enough. And not enough goes by luck, and money, and who has a pharmacist in the family.

For a while there was a fallback. When Hope's shelf ran out of the drug I used to carry, doctors switched people to the competitor's — Mounjaro, the one whose reps I'd spent years fighting for lunch slots. Then that one went short too. Musical chairs across brands, and every switch was a new prior authorization, a new starting dose, a new month of nausea, one more call for Hope to make. Somewhere in there, the rivalry I had taken personally for a decade stopped meaning anything. There wasn't enough of anybody's drug.

---

Nine at night, off the clock, Hope was on the phone with the store two towns over. A man had been to four pharmacies that day, still in his work boots, his numbers bad enough that a fifth no would have been a medical event. Hers was the first counter where somebody looked past the screen.

Another week, a woman with one pen left and nothing coming. Hope got her prescriber on the phone, got a bridging plan blessed, and then, in the ten feet between the counter and the door, showed her exactly how to make the pen last until the truck came. The woman was going to stretch it either way. The only question was whether it would be safe.

And the anger. A man slammed his empty pen on the counter and told her she was hiding stock in the back. She let him say it. There was nothing in the back.

That Thursday, coat still zipped, she said one more thing:

"The ones who scare me aren't the ones who yell. It's the ones who say 'okay' and walk out. Because I don't know what they go home and do."

Some of them went home and did nothing, and their numbers climbed. Some went back on insulin — back to the needle, and for some of them the needle was never just medicine. It was the first day of the end, and they had only just stopped believing that. And nobody in the whole chain was assigned to tell them: *the system ran out, not you.*

Nobody's job. So mostly, nobody said it.

I had asked a hundred doctors one question by then. Hallways, sample closets, parking lots. It took me until her coat finally came off that night to ask her.

"Who are you worried about?"

She looked at me the way the doctor in the hallway had, years before. Like nobody had asked her that all year either.

Then she told me. Eleven noes, and she could still name most of them.

"It's not your fault either," I said. "You didn't run out. The system did."

She's a pharmacist. She knows the supply chain better than I do. She didn't believe me either.

So I said it again.

---

The shortage ended the way shortages do. New plants, new supply, an announcement in early 2025 that most patients never heard. Everyone stopped talking about it.

Hope still comes home some Thursdays and sits down with her coat on. I don't ask how many.
