I had a lunch scheduled with an important physician in Pace, Florida. I was carrying it in myself — that's the arrangement; the rep brings lunch, the office gives minutes — and there was another rep already standing in the office. You size up another rep's bag on instinct. I asked what product he had.

"Compounded Ozempic," he said.

I sat back, and the thought arrived in exactly these words: *this man is an illegal drug dealer.*

Because Ozempic is a patented medication. There is no such thing as compounded Ozempic. There's the drug — made in factories the FDA walks through, sold in a sealed pen I was federally accountable for down to the box — and there's something else wearing its name. And the something else had reps now. Detailing the same offices. Bringing lunch to my doctors.

That was the supply side, standing in a hallway I'd worked for years. The demand side found me at a cookout.

A friend of a friend heard I worked for Novo Nordisk and crossed the yard holding her phone like it was evidence. On the screen was a glass vial with a printed label. Semaglutide, it said. Two hundred dollars a month. A telehealth visit that took eleven minutes. She hadn't been able to get Ozempic for four months — the pharmacy kept saying backordered — and her insurance had stopped covering it anyway.

"You sold this stuff," she said. "Is this the same thing?"

I looked at her phone for a long time.

When I carried the bag, I could tell you where every milligram came from. The pen came sealed. The pharmacist scanned it. The lot number traced back to a factory the FDA had walked through. If something went wrong, there was a phone number, a recall system, a person whose job it was to answer.

The vial on her screen came from a compounding pharmacy she couldn't name, prescribed by a clinician she'd never met, shipped in a padded envelope with syringes she was supposed to fill herself.

"I don't know," I said. "That's the honest answer."

She looked at me the way patients look at doctors when the appointment is ending and the real question hasn't been asked yet.

---

Here is what had happened while I was in other rooms.

The drug worked, and then the internet found out it worked, and then everyone wanted it. Demand blew past anything the factories could do. By 2022 the FDA put semaglutide on the official shortage list, and that triggered a rule written for hospitals with broken supply lines: when a drug is in shortage, compounding pharmacies are allowed to make copies of it.

Nobody wrote that rule imagining a gold rush.

Almost overnight, the vial on her phone was for sale everywhere. Med spas sold it next to Botox. Websites sold it after a questionnaire. Trainers whispered about it at gyms. Some compounders were careful, licensed, honest. But plenty of what sold under the name semaglutide never went near a licensed pharmacy at all — salt forms of the molecule that had never been through a clinical trial, wrong doses, vials from nowhere. And alongside all of it a third stream: counterfeit Ozempic pens started showing up in pharmacies in 2023, and some of those contained insulin, which put people in hospitals with crashing blood sugar. Poison control lines lit up with callers who had drawn up ten times their dose — because the pen they'd been denied did the measuring for you, and the padded envelope that replaced it did not.

I sold the branded version at more than nine hundred dollars a month. So I don't get to lecture anybody. The woman at the cookout had done the math her insurance company left her with: the real thing she couldn't get or couldn't afford, or two hundred dollars from a pharmacy she couldn't visit. The compounders didn't invent that math. They just answered the phone.

---

At the cookout I did the only thing I knew how to do. I stopped being a guy at a cookout and became a rep again for about ten minutes. Who prescribed it. What pharmacy. "Did anyone teach you to draw it up?"

"There's a video," she said.

A video. That's who was in the room with her. A video.

"Does your regular doctor know?"

She looked at the grass. "He'd give me the look," she said. She put the phone back in her pocket like she was hiding it from me too.

Years after a woman sat on an exam table apologizing for her disease, here was another one, hiding her medicine from her own doctor.

"Tell him," I said. "You didn't do anything wrong. The list price on the real thing paid my mortgage for years — you don't owe anybody an apology, least of all me. And if he gives you the look, find a different doctor."

She nodded, the way people nod when they're deciding whether to believe you. Somebody called her name from the grill.

I never found out how her story ended. I could have. It was a cookout; somebody in that yard had her number. I gave her ten good minutes and went back to my plate.

The shortage officially ended in early 2025 and the copies were supposed to stop, and mostly they did, and some just moved further into the shadows. I was back in hospice rooms by then, telling families that showing up is the whole job.

She got ten minutes from me. Same as the telehealth guy.

I still wonder what was in her vial.
