By the time the drug I used to sell got famous, I had been out of the bag for years.

I found out how famous it was at a cookout. A friend of a friend heard I used to work for Novo Nordisk and crossed the yard holding her phone like it was evidence. On the screen was a glass vial with a printed label. Semaglutide, it said. Two hundred dollars a month. A telehealth visit that took eleven minutes. She hadn't been able to get Ozempic for four months — the pharmacy kept saying backordered — and her insurance had stopped covering it anyway.

"You sold this stuff," she said. "Is this the same thing?"

I looked at the vial for a long time.

When I carried the bag, I could tell you where every milligram came from. The pen came sealed. The pharmacist scanned it. The lot number traced back to a factory that the FDA had walked through. If something went wrong, there was a phone number, a recall system, a person whose job it was to answer.

The vial on her phone came from a compounding pharmacy she couldn't name, prescribed by a clinician she'd never met, shipped in a padded envelope with syringes she was supposed to fill herself.

"I don't know," I said. "That's the honest answer. Nobody knows. That's the problem."

She looked at me the way patients look at doctors when the appointment is ending and the real question hasn't been asked yet.

---

Here is what had happened while I was in other rooms.

The drug worked, and then the internet found out it worked, and then everyone wanted it — not just the woman on the exam table with nine years of climbing numbers, but everyone. Demand did what demand does. By 2022 the FDA put semaglutide on the official shortage list, and that triggered a rule most people had never heard of: when a drug is in shortage, compounding pharmacies are allowed to make copies of it.

The rule exists for good reasons. Hospitals need it when supply breaks.

But nobody wrote that rule imagining a gold rush.

Almost overnight, semaglutide was for sale everywhere. Med spas sold it next to Botox. Websites sold it after a questionnaire. Gyms sold it out of mini-fridges. Some compounders were careful, licensed, honest. Plenty weren't. Some vials contained salt forms of the molecule that had never been tested in a human body. Some contained the wrong dose. Some — the counterfeit pens that started showing up in 2023 — contained insulin, which can kill you. Poison control lines lit up with people who had drawn up ten times their dose because nobody had taught them to read a syringe.

It was the Wild West, and like the Wild West, the people who got hurt were mostly the people who had come looking for a better life.

I want to be careful here, because the easy version of this story is a lecture, and I didn't earn a lecture. I sold the branded version at more than nine hundred dollars a month. The woman at the cookout wasn't a fool. She was doing the math her insurance company had left her with: the real thing she couldn't get or couldn't afford, or the two-hundred-dollar vial from nowhere. Desperation is a market. It always has been. The compounders didn't invent the desperation. They just showed up for it faster than anyone else did.

That's the part that stays with me. Not the gray-market chemistry — the showing up. A woman couldn't get her medicine, and the system's answer was a busy signal, and somebody's answer was an eleven-minute video call and a padded envelope. Wrong answer, maybe. But it was an answer, and it arrived. The pharmacies with the real drug, the insurers with the formularies, the offices with the fifteen-minute slots — none of them called her back.

People don't buy mystery vials because they're careless. They buy them because nobody they trust is in the room.

---

At the cookout I did the only thing I knew how to do. I stopped being a guy at a cookout and became a rep again for about ten minutes — the good parts of the job, the parts that were never on the call sheet.

I asked her questions. Who prescribed it. What pharmacy. Whether anyone had taught her to inject it, or just shipped it. Whether her doctor — her real doctor — knew she was taking it. That one landed hard. Her real doctor didn't know. She'd been afraid to say. Afraid of the look. Nine years after a woman sat on an exam table apologizing for her disease, here was another one, hiding her treatment from her own physician because shame is cheaper to ship than insulin and arrives just as fast.

"Tell your doctor," I said. "Not because you did something wrong. Because you didn't. You did what the system left you. Say exactly that. If the look comes, get a different doctor."

I don't know what was in her vial. The shortage officially ended in early 2025 and the copies were supposed to stop, and mostly they did, and some just moved further into the shadows, the way things do when a boomtown empties out. I never found out how her story ended. I was back in hospice rooms by then, where the questions are different and the vials are all accounted for.

But I think about her when people ask me why I left sales for the work I do now. Both jobs, it turns out, are about the same thing. Somebody is scared and alone with a decision that's too big for them, and the entire system is a busy signal, and the whole job — the entire job — is to be the one who shows up and asks the questions no one else is asking.

The Wild West wasn't a chemistry problem. It was a presence problem.

It usually is.
