# The Keynote

*A scene from the pharma years — Tom Brady at the national sales meeting.*

---

They flew a few thousand of us to Orlando and gave us lanyards and put Tom Brady on a stage.

That's what a national sales meeting is, if you've never been to one. A hangar-sized ballroom. Music too loud for nine in the morning. Your region's name on a screen the size of a highway billboard, and somewhere between the awards and the new quota, a famous person walks out to tell you about greatness.

I was near the peak of my numbers that year. Close to the stage. Good seats were something you earned.

He was better at it than I expected. They always are — that's the job, reading a room. He told the story everybody comes to hear: six quarterbacks taken ahead of him, the 199th pick, the scouting report that said he couldn't move. How he kept the list of doubters. How preparation beats talent when talent doesn't prepare. Thousands of salespeople stood up and roared, and I stood up too, because I was a wrestler once, and that story lives in my body: being counted out, and staying anyway.

Then we all sat down, and he said something about team — I couldn't quote it now — and my mind did the thing minds do in arenas.

It left.

It went to a hallway in New Jersey. A doctor against a wall. A woman I'd never met who cried in a parking lot where everyone could see her and no one ever went. I remember thinking: nobody in this room is going to stand and roar for her. There's no screen big enough for her name, and I didn't even know her name.

The greatest of all time was up there saying that nobody gets there alone, and eleven thousand feet of ballroom carpet away, the people we existed for were sitting in fifteen-minute slots, apologizing to their doctors.

I don't say this to be sour about it. I stood. I clapped. I liked my plaque. The man on the stage earned every ring the hard way, and there are worse things to tell a room full of salespeople than *prepare like you're 199*. It wasn't his speech that unsettled me.

It was the roar. How easy it came. How good it felt. How the whole machine of the meeting — the lights, the quota, the champion — was built to point that roar at a number.

A wrestler learns early that the roar is not the match. The match happened at six in the morning, in a cold room, with nobody watching. By the time anybody cheers, the real thing is already over.

I flew home with a signed something in my bag and a new territory plan, and on the plane I made a list of my own. Not doubters. Doctors. The ones I'd been calling on for months who were drowning and had never once been asked a real question. It was the closest thing to a scouting report I ever wrote: who's behind, who's alone in it, who's worth showing up for when there's no meeting and no music.

Years later, in hospice, I finally worked in rooms with no roar at all. Just a bed, a family, a window. Nobody claps. Nothing is measured that fits on a screen.

Brady was right about the part that mattered, though, and I think about it more now than I did in the arena: nobody gets there alone.

Nobody leaves alone either. That part was mine to learn.
