# Type One, Type Two

I didn't know the difference between type 1 and type 2 diabetes, and I was about to start training.

That's not a figure of speech. I didn't know. I could not have told you which one the drug in my future bag was for, or why it mattered. I nodded through the interview like a man who knew. Then I sat in my car in the parking lot and looked it up on my phone.

Here's why a company would hire a man like that. There was a time when pharmaceutical and device companies hired the knowledgeable — pharmacists, nurses, clinical people. They knew everything. They weren't selling. Somewhere along the line the industry ran the experiment backwards: take a good salesperson and teach him the science. And, wow, it worked. I'm what it looks like when it works.

Here's what I did know. I knew how to get up at six and work when nobody was watching. I knew how to lose in front of a gym full of people and come back the next week. I knew how to walk into a room I had no business being in and stay there until I belonged. Wrestling teaches you almost nothing about the pancreas.

The gap was three months wide, and it had a wall at the end of it: a diabetes exam. Ninety percent or better. Two chances. Miss twice, lose the job I'd just talked my way into. So I went from not knowing which type children mostly get to reciting mechanism of action, pancreatic function, glucagon, every diabetes medication on the market — GLP-1s before almost anyone outside an endocrinology office had heard the letters. I studied like it was weight cutting. Flashcards at the kitchen table. Saying it out loud in the car until it stopped sounding memorized.

And somewhere in those weeks, the two types stopped being test answers.

Type 1: the body stops making insulin. An autoimmune ambush — it just happens, often to kids, not only to kids, and nothing anyone ate or didn't eat caused it. Without insulin from outside, you die. Type 1 gets its own cruelty — strangers still ask what the kid ate — but the doctors don't blame you.

Type 2: the body still makes insulin and slowly stops listening to it. It creeps in over years. It runs in families, it rides on a hundred things — some you can pick, most you can't; the blame doesn't make that distinction. Strangers blame you. Doctors sometimes do. Worst of all, the people who have it do.

The drug I'd be carrying was for type 2. Which means the disease I'd be working in was the one that comes wrapped in shame.

I didn't understand that part from the binder. I understood it on an early ride-along, in a waiting room, listening to a man tell the front desk — unprompted, apologizing to a clipboard — that he'd been "bad this month."

Nobody answered him. Not the front desk, not the room, not me.

I had studied the mechanism of the disease for weeks. Nobody had said a word about that.

Somewhere in New Jersey there were patients on the other end of my bluff. I didn't think about them in the parking lot. I think about them now. In hospice, nobody arrives knowing what to do. Not the families, not the patients — half the time, that first year, not me. You show up not knowing, and you stay anyway, and you learn it the way I learned the pancreas: out loud, badly, until it's yours.
