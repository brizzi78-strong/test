When Novo Nordisk hired me to sell diabetes care, I did not know the difference between type 1 and type 2 diabetes.

That's not a figure of speech. I didn't know. I could not have told you which one children get, which one the drug in my future bag was for, or why it mattered. I nodded through the interview like a man who knew. Then I sat in my car in the parking lot and looked it up on my phone.

Here's what I did know. I knew how to get up at six and work when nobody was watching. I knew how to lose in front of a gym full of people and come back the next week. I knew how to walk into a room I had no business being in and stay there until I belonged. Wrestling teaches you almost nothing about the pancreas. It teaches you everything about the gap between where you are and where you need to be, and what to do about it.

The gap was wide. Training was weeks of it — binders, modules, tests, a trainer who could smell a bluff from the back of the room. I studied like it was weight cutting. Flashcards at the kitchen table. Saying it out loud in the car until it stopped sounding memorized.

And somewhere in those weeks, the two types stopped being test answers.

Type 1: the body stops making insulin. An autoimmune ambush — it just happens, often to kids, and nothing anyone ate or didn't eat caused it. Without insulin from outside, you die. Nobody calls type 1 your fault.

Type 2: the body still makes insulin and slowly stops listening to it. It creeps in over years. It runs in families, it rides on a hundred things you can't pick — and everybody calls it your fault. Strangers do. Doctors sometimes do. Worst of all, the people who have it do. The science says genetics load the gun. The world hands every type 2 patient the blame anyway, free of charge, with refills.

The drug I'd be carrying was for type 2. Which means the disease I'd be working in was the one that comes wrapped in shame.

I didn't understand that part from the binder. I understood it on an early ride-along, in a waiting room, listening to a man tell the front desk — unprompted, apologizing to a clipboard — that he'd been "bad this month." He wasn't talking to a doctor. He was confessing to furniture.

I had studied the mechanism of the disease for weeks. Nobody had said a word about that.

Years later I could deliver the science in ninety seconds while a doctor walked away from me. People assume the guys who know it cold were always the guys who knew. We weren't. Every one of us started in a parking lot with a phone, closing a gap.

I've stopped being embarrassed by how I started, because it turned out to be the right training for everything that came after. In hospice, nobody arrives knowing what to do. Not the families, not the patients — half the time, that first year, not me. You show up not knowing, and you stay anyway, and you learn it the way I learned the pancreas: out loud, badly, until it's yours.

Not knowing was never the problem.

Not showing up would have been.
