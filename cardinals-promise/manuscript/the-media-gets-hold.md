# The Media Gets Hold of Ozempic

*The pharma years — the rules I lived under, and the coverage that lived under none.*

I was not allowed to say anything. Not anything false — *anything*. Every sentence I could speak about that drug was written, reviewed, and approved before I ever said it. Every slide in my bag had been submitted to the FDA. If a doctor asked me about a use that wasn't on the label, I was trained — drilled, tested, re-tested — to stop talking and hand the question to a medical department I was not part of. Say the wrong sentence in a hallway, lose your job. Maybe worse. There were reps who wore the same suit I wore who ended up in depositions for a sentence.

The rules existed because the industry had earned them, the hard way, before my time. Neurontin. OxyContin. Reps like me helped write those rules by breaking the old ones.

One night in those years — the drug still in my bag, calls on it the next morning — I watched a morning-show clip spend four minutes on it. A chyron, a worried host, one terrifying anecdote, and a doctor who had never treated the patient in the anecdote.

Four minutes. I couldn't have legally said any of it, in either direction. At nine the next morning I'd be standing in a hallway, cleared to speak only sentences the FDA had seen first, while the television in the office waiting room said whatever it wanted about the boxes in my trunk.

That was the era when the media got hold of Ozempic, and the coverage came in waves that contradicted each other on a weekly cycle. Miracle drug. Dangerous shortcut. Hollywood's secret. Stomach paralysis. Fountain of youth. Suicide risk. Each wave true somewhere in it, or partly true, or being investigated — and each one arriving at full volume into the phone of somebody's mother.

I got the texts. Once you work in pharma you get them forever — you're the friend who sells it, which makes you the help desk. This one came on a Tuesday night, from a guy I've known twenty years: a screenshot of a headline, and under it, *should my dad stop taking this?* Then, before I could type anything: *he already skipped tuesdays dose.*

His father. Sixty-eight. Two years on the drug, the best numbers of his adult life, and one headline had already cost him a dose.

The regulators eventually looked into the scariest wave. The suicide-risk story — reviewed on two continents; neither review found a causal link. The stomach story was different: the drug really does slow the gut, and the courts are still sorting out where slow ends and stuck begins. But the suicide finding ran quiet. The scare had run loud. And between the loud story and the quiet correction, people stopped — quietly, the way you stop taking something: you just don't refill it. Some of their numbers came back up.

Some of that coverage was journalism nobody else was doing. There was reporting that year on the counterfeit pens — the ones that turned out to contain insulin, that put people in hospitals. I read every word. That story probably saved somebody, and it never scared anyone's mother. The drug deserved scrutiny; everything that powerful does. The problem was never that the media asked hard questions.

I texted back: *Don't stop anything tonight.*

Then: *Have your dad write his questions down and call the doctor who wrote it. Say a news story scared you. That's a real reason — they hear it all week.*

A minute went by. *ok. calling in the morning.*

Then: *dad wrote his questions on the back of a receipt lol*

Nobody reviewed any of it. I hit send.
