Of all the waves that came off that drug — the shortage, the craze, the scare headlines, the vials from nowhere — there was exactly one made entirely of good news, and it surprised everyone.

Babies.

It started as a whisper in online groups and then it was everywhere: women who had been told for a decade that it wasn't going to happen for them, getting pregnant. Some on purpose, once the weight came off and their cycles came back. Plenty by complete surprise — there were Facebook groups full of them, with names like *I got pregnant on Ozempic*, hundreds of women comparing due dates over a diabetes drug.

The science, once anyone looked, wasn't mysterious. Weight and insulin resistance can silence ovulation; take the weight off, and a body that had been told *not now* for fifteen years starts saying *now.* And these drugs slow the stomach — enough that one of them, Ozempic's younger cousin, carries a warning right on its label about the one small pill a lot of women were counting on.

I heard about it the way I hear about everything from that world now — sideways, at a graduation party, from a woman who found out what I used to do for a living and walked over holding her phone. I braced for the usual. The vial question, the headline question, the should-my-mother-stop question.

She showed me a sonogram.

"Eleven years," she said. "Eleven years of trying. Two rounds of the other stuff. They told us to look into adoption." She was laughing while she said it, the way people laugh when the thing is still too big to say plainly. Her husband stood behind her with the expression of a man who had stopped trying to arrange his face weeks ago.

She hadn't even started the drug to get pregnant. Her doctor had put her on it for the same reasons as everybody else. Her body did the rest.

A surprise pregnancy is only good news when it's good news. The doctors scrambled to catch up — the guidance says to stop the drug well before trying, because nobody has enough data on the drug and a growing baby, and "we don't know" is a real answer that pregnant women deserve to hear early. Some women found out late and spent months afraid — counting backward on a calendar to the doses they'd taken before the test came up positive, waiting on an ultrasound to say whether "we don't know" applied to them.

At the party, I looked at a grainy gray photograph of somebody who did not exist the year before, and I thought about every other thing that drug had put in front of me — the shortage, the vials — and how this was the first time the story had ever ended with more people in the room instead of fewer.

I was somebody's surprise once. Born to a woman who couldn't keep me, handed to a man named Lou who wanted me more than anything he ever wanted.

"Congratulations," I said.
