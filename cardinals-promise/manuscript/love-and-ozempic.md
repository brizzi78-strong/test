# Love and Ozempic

*The pharma years — Hope and I.*

I sold the drug. My wife dispensed it.

For years that was the shape of our house: the same molecule passing through both our workdays, mine in a bag in a hallway, hers in a fridge behind a counter. I talked doctors into writing it. She talked patients through taking it. I saw the disease in fifteen-minute glimpses between exam rooms. She saw it at the register, in refill gaps, in the questions people only ask a pharmacist because a pharmacist is the last person standing between them and going home confused.

We'd meet at the kitchen table at night like two scouts back from the same war reporting on different fronts.

I knew the drug's data cold. She knew what the data leaves out — that a starting dose comes with two weeks of nausea nobody mentions at the dinner party, that a prior auth can outlast a patient's courage, that the counter is where the price becomes a face. When I wanted to know if something I was selling was actually good, I didn't read the detail aid. I asked Hope. She has never once told me what I wanted to hear, which is one of the several hundred reasons I married her.

Her name is Hope. I know how that reads in a book like this. I didn't name her; I just had the sense to find her.

Here's what the two jobs taught me about the one marriage. We both spent our days in other people's worst ten feet — hers between the counter and the door, mine between the waiting room and the exam room. You can come home from that empty. For a long stretch of those years, the love between us wasn't candlelight. It was translation. It was her reading the fine print on my mother's diagnosis at our own kitchen table. It was me learning, from watching her work, that you can tell somebody no eleven times a day and still be the kindest person they talked to.

The drug got famous, and our two fronts became one front. The shortage hit her counter. The craze hit my old territory. The same word — the one I used to spell for receptionists — was suddenly in every monologue and at every dinner we went to, and we'd catch each other's eye across the table when the joke got made, because we both knew the woman it wasn't funny to. Different women. Same woman.

People ask what it's like being married to someone in the same world. It's this: nobody has to explain the day. She says a number — eleven — and I know what kind of Thursday it was. I say a name — a doctor, a drug, Dave — and she knows which year of my life we're in. Half our marriage is spoken in shorthand that would need footnotes anywhere else.

Love is a verb in our house because it had to be. Verbs were what the days left us. Make the call. Read the label. Sit back down at the table. Ask the question. Stay.

I sold Ozempic. Hope dispensed it. Somewhere in the middle of all that medicine, we kept choosing each other — refill after refill, year after year, no prior authorization required.

That last line is a joke. She'll groan at it. Thirty seconds later she'll repeat it to somebody at work.

That's us.
