*The pharma years — Hope and I.*

I sold the drug. My wife dispensed it.

For years that was the shape of our house: the same molecule passing through both our workdays, mine in a bag in a hallway, hers in a fridge behind a counter. I talked doctors into writing it. She talked patients through taking it. I saw the disease in fifteen-minute glimpses between exam rooms. She saw it at the register, in refill gaps, in the questions people only ask a pharmacist because a pharmacist is the last person standing between them and going home confused.

We'd meet at the kitchen table at night and trade the day.

I knew the drug's data cold. She knew what the data leaves out — that the first weeks come with nausea the dinner-party version skips (I didn't mention it in hallways either), and that a prior auth can outlast a patient's courage. When I wanted to know if something I was selling was actually good, I didn't read the detail aid. I asked Hope.

Her name is Hope. That's just her name.

One night at that table, years into all this, I described my mother's hand to her — the way it had started to move on its own. She didn't hesitate. "Rob, your mom has Parkinson's."

No maybe. No let's-see-what-the-doctor-says. The neurologist got there later; Hope got there in one sentence at our kitchen table, and then she stayed in the room with what the sentence did to me. That's the marriage. She will not tell me what I want to hear, and she will not leave after she tells me.

We both spent our days in other people's worst ten feet — hers between the counter and the door, mine between the waiting room and the exam room. I learned from watching her that you can tell somebody no eleven times a day and still be the kindest person they talked to. But you can also come home from that empty, and some seasons we both did, at the same time, and the shorthand carried more of the marriage than it should have.

Then the drug got famous, and the craze came to every dinner we went to. We'd catch each other's eye across the table when the joke got made, because we both knew the woman it wasn't funny to. Neither of us said anything either.

I sold Ozempic. Hope dispensed it. Somewhere in the middle of all that medicine, we kept choosing each other — refill after refill, no prior authorization required.
