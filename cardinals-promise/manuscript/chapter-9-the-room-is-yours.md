## Chapter Nine — The Room Is Yours Now

You're standing in the doorway.

You've been standing there longer than you'd admit. Maybe it's a hospital. Maybe it's the back bedroom of the house you grew up in. It doesn't matter. What matters is the person in the bed, and the fact that you can't make your feet cross the threshold.

I know why. You're not a coward. You're just afraid of the same thing everybody's afraid of. You think you're supposed to walk in there with the right words. You think there's a correct thing to say to a person who is dying, and that everyone knows it but you, and that if you go in unarmed you'll fail them at the one moment that can never be redone.

Let me take that off your back right now.

There are no right words. There never were. The people you're picturing — the ones who seem to know what to say — they don't know either. They just went in anyway.

That's the whole secret. That's the thing I've learned from more bedsides than I can count. The task was never to say the perfect thing. The task is to not leave.

So walk in.

Pull up the chair. Get close. Not the polite distance — close. Take the hand if the hand is there to take. You don't have to announce yourself. You don't have to perform. You just have to arrive with your body, all the way, into the room you've been afraid of.

Now stop trying to fix it.

This is the part your whole life has trained you against. Your instinct is to solve. To manage. To ask the nurse a question, fluff the pillow, do something with your hands so you don't have to just sit in the enormity of it. Resist that. There's nothing to fix here. Fixing is over. What's left is the last and highest thing you can do for another human being, and it's the one thing nobody taught you: be present.

When you do speak, keep it short. Dying takes work, and long speeches ask too much of someone who's laboring. The sentences that matter most are almost nothing:

*I'm here.*

*You're not alone.*

*It's okay.*

*I love you.*

And when it's time — and you'll feel when it's time — the hardest and most generous sentence there is:

*You can go. I'll be okay. You can go when you're ready.*

That one costs you everything. Say it anyway. People hold on for us. They fight past the point of comfort because they think leaving is a betrayal, because they can feel us in the room white-knuckling them to stay. The bravest thing you will ever do for someone you love is open your hand and let them know the door is unlocked.

And then — this is where most people fail, so hear me — let the silence be.

You will want to fill it. Silence in that room feels unbearable, like a test you're failing in real time. It isn't. The silence is not empty. The silence is the two of you, together, at the edge of the biggest thing there is, and your only job is to not flinch away from it. Let it sit. Let it be long. Presence doesn't need a soundtrack.

I can tell you all this because I've been where you are, on both sides of it.

I sat with strangers for years. I got good at it, the way you get good at anything you do enough times. And then it was my own father in the bed — Lou, the man who drove to another country to bring home a baby that wasn't his, the man who taught me love without ever once making a speech about it — and I found out whether any of it was real.

It was real. Not because I had special words. I didn't. It was real because I already knew the one thing that counts, and now I'm handing it to you: I knew to stay. He didn't say much, that last stretch. Neither did I. We didn't need to. He had shown up in the room for me every single day of my childhood, and at the end all I had to do was give it back. Pull up the chair. Take the hand. Stay.

He died on Christmas. I had done this a hundred times for other families and it did nothing to armor me from doing it once for mine. That's the truth I owe you, so you're not blindsided: knowing how to be in that room will not spare you the grief of it. Nothing spares you the grief. But it will spare you the other thing — the regret. The specific, permanent regret of the person who stood in the doorway and never crossed it. That one you do not have to carry. That one is a choice, and it's yours to make, right now, tonight.

You are going to want to run. Some animal part of you will beg you to remember an errand, to step out for coffee, to be anywhere but here. That urge is not weakness. It's the most human thing there is. Feel it — and don't obey it. This is the circle, and everything you are gets shown inside it, and the only move is the one that's always been the move: don't leave.

You don't need to be ready. Nobody's ready. You don't need the words. There are none.

You just need to walk in, and pull up the chair, and stay.

That's it. That's the whole heroism. It won't look like much from the outside — a person, in a chair, holding a hand, saying almost nothing. But it is the bravest, most loving act available to you on this earth, and when it's over you will know you did not leave.

The room is yours now.

Go in.
