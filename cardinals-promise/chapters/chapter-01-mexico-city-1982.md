# Chapter 1: Mexico City, 1982 — The Escape

At four years old in Mexico City, my world was small and loud and warm, and I didn't know yet that it was about to end.

I remember shoes hanging from clotheslines strung between the buildings. Vendors selling food in the street. Music, always music. Our apartment was small even for someone my size — a short doorway, walls with the rough feel of stucco, the smell of tortillas and fresh laundry hanging in the air. My mother kept her own music going from the kitchen while she cooked, and when it played, I danced. I didn't decide to. The music came, and I answered it.

My cousin Marcelo had a birthday party once — the first piñata I ever saw. When it finally broke open and all the candy spilled out, I stood there, fascinated.

I was a happy child. My mother and my father, Horacio, were the reason. She would set Chris on her hip and sing "The Locomotion" in her apron, dancing the two of us around the kitchen.

She was thirty-one. Blond, striking, and unmistakably different in a country of dark hair and darker eyes. People said she looked like Sharon Stone, and they weren't wrong. She didn't shrink — not even when everything around her was designed to make a woman in her position feel small.

Five years earlier, at twenty-six, she had done something that still strikes me as almost reckless, in the best possible way. She moved to Mexico without the language or the culture, following a man she loved into a life she didn't yet understand. Then she taught herself fluent Spanish through nothing but immersion and stubbornness. A lot of it, she told me later, she picked up from soap operas.

Horacio was my biological father. He had big highs and deep lows. One day he came home so excited he scooped me off the floor — he'd gotten a promotion. *Claudio, you are my good luck charm,* he said. My mother made carne asada tacos and Mexican wedding cake. Another day he came home quiet, excused himself to the bedroom, and slept for what felt like three days. You never knew which version of him would walk through the door. My mother did everything she could. She didn't leave easily, and she didn't leave quickly. And at some point Horacio stood on the edge of his own despair and walked away from it in the only direction he could see.

When she was pregnant, I asked her to carry me. *I can't,* she said. *I'm having a baby. And it's your baby.* When she came home from the hospital holding Chris, I looked up and asked, "Is that my baby?" She smiled. "Yes. He wants you to hold him." I gave it a try, but he started crying, so I handed him back. *Mom, I'll try again later.*

Chris cried a lot as a baby — ear infections, one after another. My mother held him for hours, walking him through the apartment, trying to work out what he needed. I wanted her attention on those nights too. So I'd lie in my room with my record player, the one Horacio had bought me, and play the same song over and over.

Then my mother made a decision. She told me we were going to visit my grandfather in America.

I had no framework for what that meant. The last time I saw Horacio was in the hallway, the newspaper still in his hand. *Have fun,* he said. *I'll see you in two weeks.* My mother acted like it was nothing. She kissed him and said *I love you.* I hugged him and kissed him too. I was wearing the Mickey Mouse t-shirt he'd bought me, already a little too small. I didn't know it was goodbye — not the permanent kind.

The airport comes back to me as noise and motion and my mother's hand — always my mother's hand. She had her suitcase and she had Chris. I carried my own little red suitcase. The airplane I remember more clearly: the seatbelt enormous around my small body, the engines building into something louder than anything in a life already full of loud things. The way the whole vessel shuddered, then lifted, and then the city was below us, shrinking. My mom taught me to pop my ears, and I was sure I was going to rupture them. I blew so hard.

I don't remember being afraid. I remember trust — complete, uncomplicated, four-year-old trust that wherever we were going, she had already thought it through.

We landed in New Jersey in early winter, and everything changed at once.

The language went first. Spanish disappeared almost overnight — not gradually, not in stages. One day it was the only language I had, and then it wasn't. English closed in around me, sharp and fast and built on rules I didn't have yet. I understood nothing, then pieces, and then, slowly, the pieces became a language I lived inside — though I never quite lost the ghost of the one I was born into. I still had a lisp that would send me to speech therapy twice a week ("th," "zzzz," over and over). With the language went pieces of myself I didn't yet know how to name.

The air was colder than any I'd felt. I could see my own breath, which was new. The trees held onto orange leaves and snow covered the ground. As far as I knew, Newark Airport was simply what America looked like.

My Aunt Nancey picked us up in her little red Honda Civic and drove us to my grandparents' house in West Paterson — a quiet home, no chaos, no shouting. Just routines and still evenings that felt enormous after the constant motion of Mexico City. It was only my mother, Chris, and me now. I missed Horacio.

And my mother began the slow work of rebuilding a life from nothing — without a formal education, without guarantees, carrying two small boys and the entire weight of their future. She just worked.

This book is not her story. She deserves her own, told in her own words, and I hope she writes it someday. But this book wouldn't exist without her willingness to choose us — again and again — when it asked for a courage she never expected anyone to notice.

She gave me the first thing a person needs before they can become anything.

A chance.
